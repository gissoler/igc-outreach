GlobalData’s Clinical Trials Database shows Alzheimer’s disease pipeline activity
concentrated in early-phase trials, with Phase I and II together making up over
three-quarters of studies. The US leads global research at 32.7% of activity,
followed by China, the UK, Japan, and Canada. Late-stage attrition remains the
field’s persistent challenge.

Alzheimer’s disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that is characterised by cognitive decline, memory impairment, and functional deterioration. The disease is driven by complex pathophysiological mechanisms, including amyloid-beta plaque accumulation, tau protein aggregation, and neuroinflammation. Despite decades of research, it remains an area of high unmet need, with limited disease-modifying treatment options and significant challenges in clinical development.

According to GlobalData’s Clinical Trials Database, most activity is concentrated in the early stages of development. Phase I accounts for approximately 43.6% of all Alzheimer’s disease trials, highlighting a strong influx of early stage candidates entering the pipeline. Phase II represents 32.6% of all Alzheimer’s disease trials, indicating a reasonable progression into proof-of-concept studies. However, there is a notable drop into the later stages, with Phase III comprising 13.7% of trials and Phase IV accounting for 10% of trials.

When looking at geographies, the US accounts for approximately 32.7% of total activity, making it the clear leader in the global Alzheimer’s disease landscape. China follows with 12.1% of total activity, reflecting its growing role in clinical research. The UK represents 8.7% of total activity while Japan contributes 8.1% and Canada accounts for 7.9%. Collectively, these five countries represent around 69.5% of total Alzheimer’s disease activity, showing a highly concentrated global research footprint.

Overall, the data highlights a pipeline that is both active and rich. The strong volume of early phase trials, combined with a geographically concentrated distribution led by the US and supported by major research hubs in China, the UK, Japan, and Canada, points to sustained global investment in Alzheimer’s disease research. At the same time, the continued dominance of early stage development emphasises the persistent scientific and clinical challenges associated with translating promising mechanisms into late-stage success.

Taken together, the landscape reflects a balance between progress and difficulty. While there is clear momentum in early research and strong international participation, advancing candidates through later-stage development and achieving disease-modifying outcomes remains a key hurdle for the field.
