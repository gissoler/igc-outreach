# MedTech Funding: A Quarter-by-Quarter Analysis
Last updated: 6 October 2026

Everything we know about the MedTech market, in one place. Get our report whether you build or invest.

## SUMMARY

MedTech funding recovered strongly in the first half of 2026 before slowing sharply in Q3. Across five quarters, 245 financings generated approximately $8.54 billion, but the recovery was uneven: more startups gained access to smaller rounds while a handful of major transactions continued to dominate capital totals.

Deal activity nearly tripled from 28 financings in Q4 2025 to 76 in Q2 2026, before falling to 47 in Q3. The latest decline of 38.2% shows how quickly the early-year expansion lost momentum, although activity remains above its late-2025 low.

Q1 2026 was arguably the strongest quarter for broad-based fundraising. Deal counts nearly doubled while total capital declined slightly, and funding excluding rounds above $50 million jumped 107.9%. More companies were getting financed without depending on extraordinary investments.

The $3.17 billion funding peak in Q2 2026 was heavily distorted by Boston Scientific's $1.5 billion investment in MiRus. That single transaction represented 47.4% of quarterly funding, while capital excluding rounds above $50 million increased only 3.2%.

Typical financing sizes have decreased considerably. The median round fell from $21.45 million in Q3 2025 to roughly $10 million in Q3 2026, reflecting the expansion of smaller transactions rather than a uniform increase in available capital.

New startups benefited from the recovery, but their momentum has weakened. First financings rose from three in Q4 2025 to 19 in Q2 2026, before dropping to nine in Q3. Even at the peak, first-time recipients attracted only a small fraction of total invested capital.

Diagnostic Devices stands out as one of the more resilient categories. Its funding increased from $87.8 million in Q1 2026 to $167.4 million in Q3, despite the wider market slowdown. Meanwhile, Digital Medical Software, Medical Imaging Systems and Surgical & Robotic Systems experienced substantial reversals.

Implantable Devices remains the largest category by capital raised, but its funding is unusually concentrated. MiRus alone accounted for approximately 72% of the category's Q2 total, demonstrating how strategic investments can overwhelm underlying market trends.

Geographic leadership is shifting in relative terms. North America represented 61.7% of Q3 2026 financings, largely because Europe's deal activity fell much faster. North American transaction volume also declined, so its increasing share does not mean the region is continuously attracting more deals.

Corporate investors are becoming more visible even as overall fundraising slows. Strategic-backed transactions represented 21.3% of Q3 deals, the highest proportion in the five-quarter period, suggesting that established medical-technology companies are increasingly influential in financing promising platforms.

The main question now is whether smaller startups can secure the larger follow-on rounds required for clinical development, regulatory approval and commercialization. MedTech is still financing new ideas, but a lasting recovery will require stronger deal activity, more consistent growth rounds and less dependence on exceptional strategic transactions.

## Is MedTech funding actually recovering?

MedTech funding recovered strongly in early 2026, but the latest quarter shows that investors are already pulling back from that pace.

We tracked 245 financings worth approximately $8.54 billion across five complete quarters, from Q3 2025 to Q3 2026. Deal activity fell from 40 transactions in Q3 2025 to 28 in Q4, then climbed to 54 in Q1 2026 and 76 in Q2. By Q3, the number had dropped to 47.

The increase in early 2026 was substantial, especially given how weak the final quarter of 2025 had been. But two consecutive quarters of growth, followed by a 38.2% decline, are not enough to establish a lasting recovery.

Capital raised tells an even more uneven story. Total funding reached $3.17 billion in Q2 2026, more than twice the previous quarter's figure, before falling to $1.15 billion in Q3. Much of that volatility came from unusually large transactions.

Our calculations show that capital excluding financings above $50 million rose from $353.7 million in Q4 2025 to $735.1 million in Q1 2026, then barely increased to $758.5 million in Q2.

For now, the clearest conclusion is that MedTech financing became much more accessible during the first half of 2026, but that expansion has already started to lose momentum.

## Why did MedTech startups raise more money even as deals fell in late 2025?

MedTech investors concentrated their money in a smaller group of companies during Q4 2025, creating the appearance of a stronger funding market even as fewer startups secured financing.

Deal volume fell 30% between Q3 and Q4, from 40 to 28 transactions. Yet total capital increased nearly 20%, reaching $1.51 billion.

Eight rounds above $50 million accounted for 76.6% of that quarter's funding. Five financings reached at least $100 million, and the largest was $250 million.

Meanwhile, the median round barely moved, falling from $21.45 million to $20.25 million. Funding below the large-round threshold contracted by more than a third.

Large checks remained available, but fewer companies were getting funded overall.

The market became unusually dependent on a handful of substantial financings. This helps explain why the following quarter's recovery was so striking: investors suddenly started financing many more companies without spending more money overall.

## What changed in MedTech funding at the start of 2026?

MedTech investors started backing far more early-stage companies in Q1 2026, making it the clearest turning point in the five-quarter period.

Financings almost doubled, increasing from 28 to 54, while total capital actually declined slightly to $1.46 billion.

The expansion came from smaller transactions. Seed and Series A financings increased from 11 to 29, while first financings rose from three to 12.

We also calculated a 107.9% increase in capital excluding rounds above $50 million. That is particularly striking because the five largest financings accounted for only 40.1% of funding, down from 63.4% in the previous quarter.

There were changes across multiple categories, too. Therapeutic Devices increased from six to 22 transactions, Digital Medical Software from one to eight, and Implantable Devices from five to nine.

The increase was therefore visible across company maturity, financing sizes and several medical-technology categories.

Q1 was the strongest quarter for genuinely broader funding activity, even though it attracted less total capital than Q4.

The extraordinary MedTech funding total in Q2 2026 was largely driven by MiRus, which received a $1.5 billion strategic equity investment from Boston Scientific.

Boston Scientific announced the transaction on May 18, 2026, acquiring approximately 34% of MiRus. Its SEC filing specifies a 33.75% fully diluted equity interest and an exclusive option relating to MiRus's transcatheter aortic valve business.

The investment was closely tied to MiRus's SIEGEL valve technology, which uses a proprietary rhenium alloy and was entering a pivotal clinical trial involving up to 1,025 patients.

The deal alone represented 47.4% of the $3.17 billion recorded across MedTech that quarter.

Without financing rounds above $50 million, capital increased only 3.2% from Q1 to Q2. That gives a much better idea of how little ordinary funding changed beneath the record total.

Still, Q2 produced 76 financings, up from 54 in Q1. We counted 38 early-stage deals and 19 first financings, both higher than the previous quarter.

MiRus explains much of the extraordinary capital figure. The wider increase in financed companies was real, although it proved short-lived.

## Are MedTech startups getting smaller funding rounds now?

MedTech startups are generally raising smaller rounds than they were in late 2025, even though a few exceptionally large investments continue to dominate headline funding totals.

The median financing declined from $21.45 million in Q3 2025 to $9.65 million in Q2 2026. It then stabilized around $10 million in Q3.

One reason is the growing number of smaller deals. Financings below $20 million increased from 14 in Q4 2025 to 32 in Q1 and 47 in Q2, before falling to 29 in Q3.

The $20 million–under $50 million segment was much steadier during 2026, recording 12, 14 and 12 financings across the three quarters.

There is also an important difference between first-time recipients and companies returning for more capital. In Q3, the median first financing was just $1.5 million, compared with $16.3 million for follow-ons.

This suggests investors have become more willing to finance companies at relatively modest ticket sizes, while larger amounts remain concentrated among businesses with established funding histories.

The broader availability of small rounds was one of the clearest developments in early 2026. Whether that ultimately produces more companies capable of raising substantial growth capital remains unanswered.

## Did MedTech funding suddenly get worse in Q3 2026?

MedTech fundraising slowed sharply in Q3 2026, with fewer deals, less capital and weaker activity across several previously expanding categories.

Transactions declined from 76 to 47, while recorded capital fell from approximately $3.17 billion to $1.15 billion.

The decline looks less dramatic once we remove exceptionally large financings, but it remains meaningful. Capital excluding rounds above $50 million decreased 27% to $554 million.

The number of early-stage transactions also fell from 38 to 21. First financings dropped from 19 to nine, while Digital Medical Software went from ten transactions to three.

Europe experienced an especially sharp reversal, with MedTech deals falling from 28 to 12. North America also declined, although less severely, from 37 to 29.

There were some pockets of resilience. Diagnostic-device capital continued growing, and the median financing across the market stayed near $10 million.

Q3 still recorded considerably more deals than Q4 2025, so the market has not returned to its weakest point. But the decline is too widespread to dismiss as the disappearance of one large transaction.

## Is MedTech funding becoming concentrated around a few winners?

MedTech capital remains heavily concentrated, and the share captured by the biggest financings changes dramatically from one quarter to the next.

In Q4 2025, the five largest transactions accounted for 63.4% of all funding. That share fell to 40.1% in Q1 2026, climbed back to 61.6% in Q2 and declined to 47% in Q3.

These changes reflect different types of funding quarters.

Q4 2025 featured several large deals alongside relatively weak financing volume. Q1 2026 brought more transactions and much broader access to capital. Q2 combined a large number of financings with the exceptional MiRus investment.

Concentration is especially important in a sector where individual clinical-device platforms can attract substantial investment from established medical-technology groups.

The $140 million financing announced by SetPoint Medical in August 2025 provides another example. It comprised a $25 million second tranche of Series C and a separate $115 million Series D, co-led by Elevage Medical Technologies and Ally Bridge Group.

SetPoint said the money would support commercialization of its neuroimmune modulation system for rheumatoid arthritis and development of treatments for other autoimmune diseases.

That is a very different financing situation from an early-stage startup raising several million dollars to advance a device concept.

As seen earlier, changes in the number and size of these large transactions can dramatically reshape quarterly funding totals, even when ordinary financing activity follows a different path.

## Are investors still funding new MedTech startups?

New MedTech startups are still getting funded, but their financing activity has fallen back from the early-2026 peak.

We identified three first financings in Q4 2025, followed by 12 in Q1 2026 and 19 in Q2. The number then declined to nine in Q3.

At the peak, first financings represented one-quarter of all quarterly deals, compared with only 10.7% at the end of 2025.

Their capital share was much smaller. In Q2, companies classified as first-time recipients raised approximately $152.7 million, while follow-on recipients attracted around $3.01 billion.

This difference partly reflects the smaller sums required at earlier development stages. It also shows how strongly capital allocation remains tilted toward companies with existing financing histories.

We should be precise about the meaning of first financing here. The classification comes from documented fundraising histories and does not claim that every private investment a company previously received has been identified.

Even with that limitation, the reversal is clear. New-company financing became much more common during the first half of 2026, then weakened as the wider market slowed.

## Are MedTech investors moving away from early-stage companies?

Early-stage MedTech companies still attract a substantial share of deals, although investors continue putting most of their money into later-stage businesses.

Seed and Series A transactions represented 27.5% of deals in Q3 2025, climbed to 53.7% in Q1 2026 and reached 50% in Q2. Their share subsequently eased to 44.7% in Q3.

The capital distribution looked very different. Later-stage financings captured approximately 70.1% of Q1 2026 funding, 86.8% in Q2 and 72.5% in Q3.

More younger companies gained access to relatively modest rounds during the recovery, while businesses raising later-stage capital continued attracting much larger investments.

The combination makes sense for medical technology. Developing a device, testing it clinically, obtaining regulatory clearance and preparing it for commercialization can involve several distinct rounds of financing.

For example, SetPoint Medical's August 2025 financing was explicitly intended to support commercialization after years of development. MiRus's strategic transaction came while its SIEGEL valve was advancing through clinical evaluation.

Those transactions help explain why investors can increase the number of early-stage deals while continuing to direct most capital toward more mature opportunities.

There is no reliable evidence that the average funded company is becoming progressively older. Founding-year information is incomplete, particularly in Q3 2026, where several records contain invalid dates.

## Is diagnostic technology becoming the hottest part of MedTech funding?

Diagnostic Devices currently shows one of the clearest improvements in MedTech fundraising, with more companies getting funded and capital continuing to rise even as the wider market slowed.

Diagnostic-device deals increased from three in Q1 2026 to 13 in Q2, before settling at nine in Q3.

Capital grew throughout that sequence, from $87.8 million to $128.3 million and then $167.4 million.

The particularly interesting detail is that typical rounds remained relatively small. The category's median financing was $3.9 million in Q2 and $4 million in Q3.

That combination suggests the increase was spread across multiple relatively modest financings rather than being created by one exceptionally large deal.

The commercial environment also gives us a reason to keep watching this category. That regulatory progress provides useful context, although we cannot attribute the recent financing increase to FDA decisions without examining the milestones of each funded company.

## Why are implantable devices attracting so much money?

Implantable Devices attract some of MedTech's largest investments because certain technologies can become strategically valuable to major medical-device manufacturers long before they reach widespread commercial use.

The category raised approximately $710.6 million in Q3 2025, $442.5 million in Q4 and $385.6 million in Q1 2026. Capital then surged to $2.09 billion in Q2 before dropping to $463.8 million in Q3.

The extraordinary second-quarter increase came largely from MiRus. Its financing represented approximately 72% of the category's recorded capital that quarter.

Boston Scientific's investment gives us a concrete example of how strategic buyers approach promising medical technologies. The SEC filing describes potential additional cash payments totaling $3 billion if the option is exercised under the specified conditions.

The deal therefore combined financing with a structured path toward potential ownership of a valuable product platform.

Implantable Devices still recorded 11 financings in Q3, and the category remained the largest by capital raised. But its dollar totals are unusually sensitive to transactions of this scale.

## Is North America taking over MedTech startup funding?

North America remains the biggest region for MedTech financing and increased its share of global deals in Q3 2026, partly because Europe slowed much faster.

North American deals rose from 12 in Q4 2025 to 24 in Q1 2026 and 37 in Q2. They subsequently declined to 29 in Q3.

That 57.1% European decline was much sharper than the overall market contraction.

Asia-Pacific followed another pattern. Deal activity peaked at ten transactions in Q1 2026, then fell to eight in Q2 and four in Q3.

As a result, North America accounted for 61.7% of Q3 MedTech financings.

There is a difference between increasing market share and attracting more investment. North America actually recorded fewer deals than in Q2, despite becoming relatively more dominant.

The region's earlier capital share was also heavily influenced by unusually large American transactions, particularly MiRus.

Our figures therefore confirm sustained North American leadership in funded-company activity. They do not yet show a continuously widening advantage in absolute financing volume.

## Are corporate investors becoming more active in MedTech?

Corporate investors are appearing in a larger share of MedTech financings lately, with strategic-backed transactions reaching their highest proportion of the five-quarter period in Q3 2026.

Strategic investors participated in 7.1% of recorded deals in Q4 2025, followed by 16.7% in Q1 2026, 17.1% in Q2 and 21.3% in Q3.

The median strategic-backed financing also increased during the three 2026 quarters, from $11.75 million to $22.96 million and then $23.65 million.

The recent increase is worth taking seriously, especially because it continued during a weaker funding quarter. It remains an emerging pattern rather than a proven long-term trend.

## Are new investors entering MedTech, or are the same firms funding everything?

MedTech attracted substantially more disclosed lead-investor activity during the early-2026 recovery, but we cannot yet show that the investor base itself is becoming more diverse.

The number of identifiable lead-investor names increased from 28 in Q4 2025 to 55 in Q1 2026, before declining to 54 in Q2 and 41 in Q3.

Disclosure quality changed at the same time. We could identify lead investors for 74.1% of Q1 financings, compared with 57.9% in Q2 and 59.6% in Q3.

That makes it difficult to distinguish genuine changes in investor participation from differences in available information.

Several specialist investors appeared repeatedly. Sonder Capital was among the recurring names in Q4 2025, Arboretum Ventures and Sherpa Healthcare Partners in Q1 2026, and S3 Ventures and Medtronic in Q3.

SetPoint Medical offers a useful example of how specialist investors can work together on substantial rounds. Its August 2025 Series D was co-led by Elevage Medical Technologies and Ally Bridge Group, with participation from several new and returning investors.

The structure shows that established specialist investors can remain involved as companies approach commercialization.

We have stronger evidence that investor activity rose alongside financing volume than that entirely new pools of capital entered MedTech. The latter would require more complete investor identities and funding histories.

## Are MedTech startups struggling to raise bigger growth rounds?

MedTech has a visible gap between the large number of smaller financings and the relatively limited number of substantial growth rounds. Whether that reflects an actual funding shortage is harder to prove.

During the first half of 2026, the increase in smaller deals was much stronger than the increase in mid-sized transactions.

By Q3, 29 financings were below $20 million, while only 12 were between $20 million and $50 million. There was just one financing of at least $100 million.

Capital requirements often increase substantially as medical-technology companies move toward pivotal clinical studies, regulatory submissions, manufacturing and commercialization.

The FDA's regulatory framework illustrates some of those development requirements. Depending on the product, a company may need to obtain 510(k) clearance, De Novo authorization or premarket approval. More demanding clinical and regulatory pathways can involve considerable development work before a device reaches the market.

Large private financings can help companies cross those stages. SetPoint Medical specifically linked its $140 million raise to commercialization and pipeline development, while MiRus received strategic capital alongside the progression of its transcatheter valve program.

But the database only captures completed financings. It does not record every unsuccessful fundraising attempt or how much money companies originally sought.

The concern is the narrow and inconsistent flow of larger rounds relative to smaller financings. We cannot yet calculate how many otherwise viable companies are being held back by a shortage of capital.

## What is MedTech funding really telling us right now?

MedTech funding remains active, but the recovery has become less convincing after a sharp third-quarter slowdown. The strongest underlying change is that smaller startups gained much better access to financing during early 2026, even though large and established companies continued attracting most of the capital.

The early-2026 recovery was broad. More companies raised money, early-stage deals increased and several categories expanded together. Q1 was particularly strong because that improvement remained visible after removing the biggest transactions.

The subsequent boom in reported capital was much narrower. MiRus accounted for almost half of Q2 funding, showing how an individual strategic investment can completely change the headline picture.

Smaller rounds have become more common, but bigger growth financings remain inconsistent. This is one of the clearest areas to watch because medical-device companies often need substantial additional capital as their products approach commercialization.

Diagnostic Devices currently has the most convincing category momentum. Funding has increased across consecutive quarters, supported by elevated deal activity and relatively modest typical round sizes. Software, therapeutic devices and surgical robotics have experienced more uneven developments.

Strategic investors are another important part of the picture. Their participation reached a recent high in Q3, even as general deal activity slowed. Corporate involvement may become increasingly influential in deciding which advanced medical technologies receive substantial financing.

North America continues to lead by deal count, although its relative advantage increased recently because other regions weakened more sharply.

The most important unresolved question is whether the early-2026 expansion can resume. We would look for several consecutive quarters of higher deal counts, stronger financing outside the largest rounds, and renewed first-financing activity before concluding that MedTech has entered a lasting recovery.

For now, the market is still funding new ideas, but investors appear much more selective about where they commit substantial amounts of capital.

## OUR METHODOLOGY

This analysis examines how MedTech startup funding evolved across five complete calendar quarters, from July 1, 2025, through September 30, 2026. We analyzed 245 recorded private-company financings representing approximately $8.54 billion in disclosed capital, drawing on our compiled fundraising database and cross-checking material transactions against company announcements, investor disclosures, corporate filings and established financial reporting.

We focused on companies developing medical devices, diagnostic technologies, therapeutic and implantable devices, medical imaging, patient monitoring, surgical systems and medical software. Eligible transactions primarily involved identifiable private-company equity financings. Debt, grants, loans and public offerings were outside the intended scope. Separate financing events were counted separately, while duplicate reporting of the same transaction was checked. For example, SetPoint Medical's August 2025 announcement included a $25 million second Series C tranche and a separate $115 million Series D.

We assessed fundraising through multiple dimensions rather than headline capital alone: quarterly deal counts, disclosed amounts, median financing sizes, rounds above $50 million, funding excluding those larger transactions, first financings, financing stages, technology categories, geography and investor participation. First-financing classifications reflect documented fundraising histories, while investor participation is based on disclosed identities and roles. Undisclosed amounts were excluded from monetary calculations, and incomplete company information was excluded from analyses requiring those specific fields.

Exceptional financings received additional scrutiny. In particular, we verified Boston Scientific's May 2026 $1.5 billion investment in MiRus against its official announcement and SEC filing, including the equity interest acquired and the associated acquisition-option structure. Potential future acquisition payments were not counted as additional startup financing. We also distinguished completed financing activity from broader market demand, since announced rounds alone cannot establish how many companies attempted unsuccessfully to raise capital.

Our conclusions come from comparing fresh quarterly fundraising records, aggregating activity by financing size, company maturity, category and region, and then assessing which developments persisted or reversed. We paid particular attention to the difference between increases in funded-company activity and capital spikes caused by individual large transactions. Regulatory developments were used as industry context, not as direct evidence of investor demand.
