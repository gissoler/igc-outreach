# What’s funding like in the MedTech market right now?
Last updated: 6 October 2026

Building a startup or looking for your next investment in MedTech? Our report gives you the full picture.

## SUMMARY

MedTech funding is healthier right now because far more companies are getting financed, but the recovery is uneven: early-stage access has reopened, huge checks remain available for proven assets, and the middle of the funding ladder has become harder.

Deal activity rose 46% and the number of funded companies jumped 50%, while disclosed capital increased 52% to $7.22 billion. The recovery therefore goes well beyond one or two headline transactions.

The typical financing actually became much smaller. Median round size fell from $19.95 million to $11.88 million, largely because Seed and Series A now account for almost half of all deals.

The strongest change is happening at the entrance to the market. First financings rose from 13 to 43, their median size more than doubled to $4.75 million, and the companies receiving them are somewhat younger than before.

Series B is the awkward part of the picture. Its share of deals fell from 25.4% to 10.9%, while $20 million to $50 million rounds also lost substantial ground, suggesting that getting started is easier than financing the transition into serious scale.

MedTech has become more barbelled. Small rounds are much more common, while a handful of very large strategic and growth financings absorb an increasing share of the dollars.

MiRus's $1.5 billion Boston Scientific investment exaggerated the headline funding increase, but it did not create it. Even after removing every round above $100 million, underlying capital still grew by roughly one-third.

Category leadership depends on what we measure. Diagnostics and patient monitoring have the clearest momentum in company formation, while implantables dominate the capital charts because mature assets can command enormous strategic checks.

Geographically, North America is pulling further ahead in dollars, Europe is gaining deals but losing relative share, and Asia-Pacific has become the clearest breadth-growth story, with activity expanding sharply in China and Australia.

The main issue to watch now is graduation. MedTech is producing a much larger crop of young funded companies, but unless the Series B layer recovers, many of those companies may find that entering the market is easier than moving through it.

## Is MedTech funding actually healthier now than a year ago?

MedTech funding is healthier today mainly because far more companies are getting financed, even though the typical round has become smaller.

We counted 202 deals in the latest 12 months, up from 138, while the number of funded companies rose from 134 to 201. Total disclosed capital increased from $4.74 billion to $7.22 billion. That combination already tells us more than the funding total alone: the market added dozens of companies rather than simply giving more money to the same group.

The weaker point is round size. The median financing dropped from $19.95 million to $11.88 million. Meanwhile, the average edged up to $36.08 million because a few very large transactions pulled it higher.

So MedTech currently looks easier to enter but more uneven once companies need serious scaling capital. The overall direction is positive, although the recovery is much stronger in breadth than in the typical size of a financing.

## Would MedTech funding still look stronger without the giant rounds?

Yes, MedTech funding still grew without the megadeals, although the underlying increase is much less spectacular than the headline 52%.

Once we remove every financing above $100 million, capital still increased 32.5%, from $3.04 billion to $4.02 billion. If we go further and strip out every deal above $50 million, funding still rose 17.1%, from about $2.00 billion to $2.34 billion.

The big rounds clearly boosted the annual total, but they did not create the recovery. Underneath them, more money was still being invested.

The more telling comparison is with the 46% jump in deal count. Ordinary funding expanded much faster in number of transactions than in dollars. These days, MedTech growth is coming largely from financing more companies rather than handing substantially larger checks to the typical one.

## Are more MedTech companies actually getting funded now?

Yes, MedTech funding has broadened sharply, with 201 companies receiving capital versus 134 a year earlier.

That 50% increase in funded companies was slightly faster than the rise in deals. Only one company completed multiple financings inside the latest 12-month window, compared with four previously.

We see the same broadening below the aggregate level. Diagnostic-device financings rose from 12 to 28. Patient-monitoring deals increased from nine to 21. Asia-Pacific went from seven deals to 25.

Those examples make the expansion hard to dismiss as an artifact of a few large rounds. More parts of the MedTech market are producing fundable companies now, and more of those companies are actually getting a check.

## Are investors funding new MedTech startups again?

Yes, new MedTech companies are getting funded much more often now, and their first checks are bigger than they were a year ago.

First financings increased from 13 to 43. They represented 21.3% of deals, compared with only 9.4% in the previous period, while capital going into those initial rounds rose from roughly $77 million to $373 million.

The size of those first checks also improved. The median climbed from $2.20 million to $4.75 million, so the increase cannot be explained simply by an explosion of tiny pre-seed deals.

Company age points in the same direction. Among companies where we could establish a founding year reliably, the median company receiving its first financing was around three years old, down from 4.1 years.

Established businesses still take most of the money because clinical trials, regulatory work, manufacturing and commercialization become expensive. But the front door of MedTech venture funding is much more open today than it was a year ago.

## Why are MedTech rounds smaller now if funding is booming?

MedTech rounds look smaller mainly because far more Seed and Series A companies are being funded, while Series B has lost a huge amount of ground.

Seed jumped from 11.6% to 23.3% of deals. Series A increased from 21.0% to 25.7%. Together they now make up almost half of all financings.

Series B moved sharply the other way, dropping from 25.4% of deals to 10.9%.

That mix change drags down the overall median even when individual stages are not getting weaker. The median Seed round actually rose from $2.25 million to $3.3 million. Series C moved from $39.7 million to $50 million. Series A, by contrast, became cheaper, with its median falling from $13.7 million to $10.7 million.

So the 40% drop in the overall median needs context. A much bigger population of early-stage companies is entering the market, and that naturally pulls the typical deal size down.

## Is Series B becoming the weak spot in MedTech funding?

Yes, Series B is currently the clearest weak spot in MedTech funding.

Its share of transactions collapsed by 14.5 percentage points, from roughly one-quarter of all deals to barely one-tenth. We see the same squeeze in the size distribution: $20 million to $50 million rounds fell from 29% to 21% of disclosed transactions.

Meanwhile, activity grew on both sides. Seed expanded dramatically, while Growth Equity doubled its share of transactions and absorbed 33.5% of all current funding.

That creates an awkward financing ladder. A young device company can currently find more seed capital than before. A mature company with strong clinical, regulatory or strategic value can still attract a very large check. The tougher part appears to be the expensive middle, where companies need enough capital to move from initial validation into serious scaling.

This is probably the biggest caveat to calling the MedTech recovery fully healthy. The young cohort being created now will eventually need that middle layer of capital.

## Is MedTech funding getting too concentrated in a few huge deals?

MedTech funding is clearly more concentrated at the top now, even though the wider market is also growing.

The single largest transaction represented 20.8% of all disclosed capital, up from 13.7% for the previous period's largest deal. The top three captured 27.7%, compared with 19.1%, and the top 10 rose from 35.9% to 44.3%.

Interestingly, the number of $100 million-plus financings did not increase at all. There were 10 in each period. They simply became much more powerful in dollar terms.

MiRus is the obvious example. Boston Scientific invested $1.5 billion for an approximately 34% stake and received an exclusive option to acquire the company's TAVR system subject to further payments and milestones. That financing alone represented about one-fifth of the period's capital.

Concentration is genuinely higher today. Still, as we saw above, capital below the megadeal layer also grew. This is a broader recovery with a heavier top end, not a market being carried entirely by a few companies.

## Is the MedTech funding boom basically a MiRus story?

No. The MedTech recovery is much bigger than MiRus, although that $1.5 billion deal seriously inflates some headline comparisons.

Removing MiRus would leave roughly $5.72 billion of disclosed capital, still comfortably above the previous period's $4.74 billion. More importantly, one transaction cannot explain 67 additional funded companies.

MiRus does tell us something useful about the upper end of the market. Boston Scientific was not simply joining a conventional venture round. The company bought a large minority stake and secured an exclusive option around MiRus's balloon-expandable TAVR technology, with future economics linked to additional milestones.

We should therefore read that $1.5 billion as evidence that a strategic buyer will commit enormous capital to a sufficiently valuable mature MedTech asset. It tells us much less about what an ordinary growth-stage startup can raise.

## Are MedTech investors taking more risk now?

MedTech investors are taking more risk at company formation today, but they remain much tougher around the middle of the funding journey.

The early-stage evidence is strong. Seed activity more than doubled as a share of deals. Initial financings became much more common. The companies getting their first institutional money are also somewhat younger.

Yet that willingness to take early technical risk has not translated into an equally loose scaling market. Series B activity is much thinner, while very large pools of capital remain available once a company has developed enough clinical, regulatory or strategic value.

In practice, investors seem increasingly comfortable with two very different propositions: backing a young company cheaply enough to explore an idea, or putting serious money behind a proven asset. The uncomfortable zone is the point where a company has already consumed meaningful capital but still has a lot left to prove.

## Which MedTech categories have real momentum right now?

Diagnostic devices and patient monitoring have the strongest breadth momentum right now, while implantable devices are winning mainly in dollars.

Diagnostic-device deals increased from 12 to 28, lifting the category's deal share by 5.2 percentage points. Patient monitoring rose from nine transactions to 21.

Neither category is getting dramatically richer. The median diagnostic financing dropped around 70%, and the patient-monitoring median fell around 58%. Investors are backing many more companies, usually with smaller amounts.

Implantables show almost the reverse pattern. Their number of transactions increased only slightly, from 38 to 42, and their share of all MedTech deals actually fell. Yet the category gained six percentage points of capital share.

Digital Medical Software looks more balanced. It gained both deal and capital share without an extreme change in check size. Medical imaging also improved modestly, including a roughly 30% increase in its median financing.

The distinction is useful: diagnostics and monitoring have genuine company-level breadth, while the implantable-device boom is much more dependent on expensive assets at the top.

## Are implantable medical devices really booming?

Implantable medical devices are booming in capital terms, but there is no comparable boom in the number of funded implantable companies.

Implantables accounted for almost 47% of current MedTech capital even though their deal share fell by 6.7 percentage points. Their funding-intensity ratio rose from 1.48 to 2.25, meaning they captured more than twice the amount of capital their deal share alone would imply.

MiRus explains a large piece of that divergence. As pointed out above, Boston Scientific's transaction was a $1.5 billion strategic investment tied to a potentially valuable structural-heart platform.

That makes the category look spectacular in dollar charts. If we are trying to understand where new company formation is strongest, diagnostics and patient monitoring are more convincing. If we want to know where strategics will pay heavily for proven technology, implantables currently stand out.

## Has MedTech funding turned into a barbell market?

Yes. MedTech funding now looks distinctly barbelled: there are many more very small rounds, while giant financings capture a bigger portion of the money.

Deals below $5 million increased from 15.9% to 27.0% of disclosed transactions. At the other end, $100 million-plus rounds captured 49.8% of capital, up from 40.1%.

The middle lost ground. Financings between $20 million and $50 million fell eight percentage points as a share of transactions.

That pattern lines up almost perfectly with the stage data. More Seed deals are being done, Series B is much less common, and mature Growth Equity rounds are absorbing a much larger pool of dollars.

For founders, the market can therefore feel completely different depending on where they sit. Early money is more available. Huge checks still exist for proven companies. Somewhere between those two points, investors are clearly more selective.

## Is Europe losing the MedTech funding race to North America and Asia?

Europe is losing relative ground in MedTech funding right now, even though European companies are still closing more deals than a year ago.

European financings increased from 58 to 69. In a slower global market that would look healthy. But worldwide MedTech activity grew so much faster that Europe's share of deals fell from 42.0% to 34.2%, while its share of capital dropped from 29.5% to 20.6%.

The European median financing also fell from $14.1 million to $7.5 million.

North America's story is different. Its share of deals stayed around 50%, but its share of capital increased eight percentage points to almost 70%. The region did not take over more of the company count; it took over more of the dollars.

Asia-Pacific made the clearest gain in breadth. Its deal share increased from 5.1% to 12.4%, driven partly by China going from two deals to eight and Australia from one to six.

Europe is aware of some of the friction around its medical-device framework. The European Commission proposed a targeted simplification of EU medical-device rules in late 2025, explicitly aiming to make the system faster, easier to navigate and more supportive of innovation. EUDAMED also became mandatory for several modules in 2026. Those changes are still too recent to explain the financing numbers we see today, but they show that regulatory burden and competitiveness are live policy issues rather than abstract complaints from the industry.

## Why are more MedTech deals happening in Asia-Pacific now?

Asia-Pacific MedTech funding is growing because many more companies are reaching investors, especially in China and Australia.

We found 25 APAC financings in the latest period versus only seven previously. The increase is mainly a breadth story because median round sizes did not surge with the deal count.

China offers one plausible explanation for part of that pipeline. Its National Medical Products Administration has recently put medical robots, high-end imaging, AI-powered medical devices and novel biomaterials among the technologies receiving special attention under measures designed to improve review, approval and whole-life-cycle regulation.

The overlap with currently active funding categories is striking. We should still be careful with causality: a policy announcement does not automatically produce venture rounds. But when regulators explicitly prioritize the same high-end device technologies in which company formation is accelerating, the policy environment becomes relevant to the financing story.

Australia's increase adds another useful clue. APAC growth is not confined to one Chinese policy cycle. More companies across the region are showing up in the funding market.

## Are corporate investors taking over MedTech funding?

No, corporate investors are not taking over MedTech funding, although they are playing a bigger role in some strategically important deals.

Strategic-backed transactions increased from 30 to 34, but the overall market expanded much faster. Their participation rate therefore fell from 21.7% to 16.8%.

The median strategic-backed round also declined from $26.0 million to $21.5 million.

Where corporate behavior changed is at the ends of the maturity curve. Strategic participation at Seed increased from one deal to six, while Growth Equity went from one to five. Series C strategic deals fell from eight to three.

MiRus shows the mature end in its most extreme form. At the other end, early strategic checks can give corporates visibility into emerging technologies long before acquisition becomes realistic.

The investor base itself also broadened, although not as quickly as the company base. We identified 196 unique disclosed investor entities versus 160 previously, while repeat investor participation became slightly less common. Medtronic was particularly visible in our current-period investor fields, appearing seven times across disclosed lead or strategic positions.

Investor disclosure is incomplete, so we would not use the absence of any particular fund or corporate name to declare that it has left the market. What we can say confidently is that the surge in funded companies has not been matched by an equally large surge in identifiable investors.

## Do MedTech companies need more proof to raise money now?

The proof required to raise MedTech money currently depends heavily on how much capital a company is asking for.

At the early stage, the market is plainly willing to fund companies before they have years of commercial proof. New-company financings have surged, and those companies are entering the funding system younger.

For large rounds, clinical and regulatory progress still carries enormous weight. HighLife is a good recent example. The company raised more than $90 million after receiving CE Mark approval for its transcatheter mitral-valve replacement system, with the new capital earmarked for European commercialization and its U.S. pivotal study.

MiRus offers another version of the same pattern. Boston Scientific's option economics depend on further milestones rather than an unconditional future acquisition.

The wider regulatory backdrop is becoming somewhat friendlier to companies that have strong technology but still need help crossing the commercialization gap. The FDA's TAP program expanded across all Offices of Health Technologies in 2026 and had 133 devices enrolled by July. The FDA and CMS also launched the RAPID coverage pathway for eligible Breakthrough Devices.

We cannot turn those examples into a clean market-wide percentage because milestone reporting is not consistently structured across deals. Still, the pattern at the upper end is fairly clear: once the check becomes large, investors want something concrete to underwrite.

## Is MedTech finally moving from R&D into commercialization?

MedTech is funding more commercialization today, but the market is also creating enough new startups that R&D remains a major part of the story.

The latest stage mix contains both forces. Almost half of deals are Seed or Series A, where product development, technical validation and early clinical work still dominate. At the same time, substantial Growth Equity capital is going into companies facing pivotal studies, manufacturing, market launches or geographic expansion.

HighLife captures that second side neatly. Its recent financing followed CE Mark approval and is being used to expand commercially in Europe while advancing a U.S. pivotal trial.

Digital health is also getting new routes into real-world deployment. The FDA launched its TEMPO pilot for digital health devices and selected its first participant in 2026. Separately, CMS's ACCESS model started operating in 2026 with an outcome-based payment structure for technology-supported chronic care, and more than 160 organizations had joined by September.

Those programs are fresh, so it would be premature to credit them with the funding increase we already measured. They do, however, make today's commercialization environment meaningfully different from the one companies faced several years ago.

MedTech capital is now flowing into both creation and deployment. We are seeing a larger crop of early companies at the same time as mature devices move through regulatory approval, pivotal studies and commercial rollout.

## Is the MedTech funding recovery happening every month, or was there one crazy spike?

MedTech funding is running at a higher monthly baseline now, although one exceptional financing window made the annual numbers look even stronger.

Using 12 equal one-month windows, the median number of deals increased from 12 to 16. Median monthly capital rose from $341 million to $523 million. Neither period contained a zero-deal month.

The peak was much larger this time. The strongest current window produced 33 deals and $2.04 billion, compared with 16 deals and $815 million at the previous peak. MiRus was a major contributor to that spike.

But the medians are more useful here than the maximums. They tell us the typical month improved as well.

The current MedTech market therefore has a stronger base as well as much bigger upside spikes. Looking only at the annual total would overstate the second effect and miss the first.

## Are established MedTech companies still taking almost all the money?

Yes, established MedTech companies still take most of the capital, even though new startups have benefited more from the recent improvement in access to funding.

Follow-on financings accounted for around $6.8 billion, roughly 94% of current disclosed capital. Their median size was $17.5 million, several times the median first financing.

That concentration is understandable in MedTech. A company entering pivotal studies, building manufacturing capacity or preparing a commercial launch can consume far more money than a team still developing an initial device.

What has changed is the bottom of the funnel. As seen above, initial financings are growing far faster than follow-ons. Established winners still dominate the dollar pool, but they no longer explain most of the growth in the number of companies being funded.

The unanswered question is what happens when today's young cohort needs its next $20 million or $30 million. The current weakness around Series B suggests that graduation could be harder than entry.

## Are MedTech companies raising again faster and at bigger step-ups?

We cannot reliably say that MedTech companies are raising again faster or achieving better round step-ups because public previous-round data is too incomplete for a defensible comparison.

Funding velocity requires the date of each company's preceding financing. Step-up analysis also needs the amount of that previous round. Those fields could not be established consistently enough across the two cohorts.

The fact that only one company completed multiple financings inside the latest 12-month window is interesting, but it does not solve the problem. A company could easily have raised immediately before the window opened and returned to market a few months later.

Stage medians do not solve it either. A $50 million Series C median today versus $39.7 million previously compares different companies; it tells us nothing about how much the same company stepped up from its Series B.

So this is one part of MedTech funding where we would rather leave a gap than manufacture a clean-looking answer from incomplete histories.

## What really changed over the last 12 months?

MedTech funding is fundamentally healthier today because access widened. The clearest change is the jump from 134 to 201 funded companies, backed by materially higher deal activity even after we strip out the biggest transactions.

The recovery is also unusually barbelled. Early-stage funding has reopened, huge growth and strategic checks remain available, but the conventional scaling middle is thinner. Series B is currently the part of the financing ladder we would watch most closely.

Capital became more concentrated even as company breadth improved. Both things happened at once: many more companies got funded while a few exceptional transactions took an unusually large slice of the dollars.

New startups gained the most in access to capital, while established companies still captured most of the money. Those are different questions, and mixing them together would hide one of the most important changes in the market.

Category momentum also needs to be separated into breadth and dollars. Diagnostics and patient monitoring are producing many more funded companies. Implantables are attracting extraordinary amounts of capital, but much of that strength comes from expensive mature assets rather than a surge in startup formation.

Geographically, North America has become even more dominant in dollars without taking more of the deal count. Europe is still funding more companies in absolute terms but is losing relative share. Asia-Pacific is the clearest emerging breadth story.

The most misleading headline would be that a 52% increase in MedTech funding means the typical company can raise much more money today. The opposite happened to the overall median. What improved most was the number and variety of companies able to get funded.

## OUR METHODOLOGY

This analysis answers the question “What’s funding like in the MedTech market right now?” by comparing fundraising announced from October 8, 2025 through October 7, 2026 with the immediately preceding period from October 8, 2024 through October 7, 2025. The two periods are consecutive, equal in length and do not overlap.

We built and normalized our own MedTech funding dataset rather than relying on the output of a single database. We researched financing announcements across company and investor releases, regulatory and government records, reputable financial and industry reporting, and established funding sources, cross-checking material facts when more than one credible source was available.

We used a strict MedTech operating-market definition. Companies had to be primarily developing medical devices, device-led diagnostic or monitoring systems, surgical or robotic medical platforms, implantable technologies, medical imaging systems or qualifying device-oriented medical software at the time of financing. Borderline companies were excluded rather than included simply because healthcare was one of several markets they served.

For funding totals, we counted qualifying private-company equity financings and isolated the equity value whenever a transaction contained several financing components. Debt, grants, loans and other non-equity capital were excluded. We did not estimate undisclosed deal amounts, and approximate figures or clear lower bounds were kept approximate rather than turned into falsely precise numbers.

Each genuinely distinct financing was counted once. Duplicate reports of the same round were consolidated, while extensions, additional closes and tranches were reviewed individually to determine whether they represented separate financings or additional reporting around the same transaction.

Stages, investors, company age, financing history, milestones and use of funds were recorded only where public evidence supported them. We did not infer stages from round size, assume that the most prominent investor was the lead, or reconstruct previous rounds from weak evidence. This is also why funding velocity and company-level round step-ups are not presented as firm conclusions.

Unusual structures were kept visible rather than treated like ordinary venture rounds. Boston Scientific's $1.5 billion investment in MiRus is included because it is a genuine equity investment, but its roughly 34% strategic stake and acquisition option are separately discussed because the transaction is not directly comparable with a conventional financing.

We also ran a separate quality-control pass covering date boundaries, duplicate financings, company identity, market eligibility, financing type and deal-size treatment. To test whether the annual increase was being distorted by a small number of outsized rounds, we compared headline capital with totals excluding financings above $50 million and $100 million.

Key sources used for transaction, regulatory and commercialization checks include Boston Scientific's announcement of its strategic investment in MiRus , Boston Scientific's investor materials on the MiRus transaction , HighLife Medical's financing announcement , China's NMPA measures supporting high-end medical-device innovation , the FDA's TAP program , the FDA and CMS RAPID coverage pathway , the FDA's TEMPO digital-health pilot , CMS's ACCESS model , and the European Commission's medical-device regulatory framework and simplification work .
