# Wearable Technology Funding Tracker (110 deals)
Last updated: 14 September 2026

Building a startup or looking for your next investment in wearable technology? Our report gives you the full picture.

## SUMMARY

The Wearable Technology Funding Tracker (110 deals) shows a market that is well funded at the top, but much smaller for the typical startup: known equity capital reaches at least $3.37 billion, while the median disclosed round is only $8.92 million.

The headline total is unusually concentrated. Deals above $50 million represent just 11.9% of financings but absorb 75.7% of known capital; remove them and the market falls to roughly $820 million.

The company base is also narrower than the number of funding announcements suggests. The dataset contains 109 qualifying financing events across 87 companies, and 82.8% of those companies appear only once during the research window.

Repeat fundraising is where the market starts to separate quickly. Only 15 companies raised more than once, yet they account for roughly 59.7% of all known capital, while follow-on financings collectively absorb about 96.6%.

Smart Wearables and Smart Glasses are the only categories with both meaningful company counts and meaningful financing depth. Smart Wearables hold more capital, largely because of Oura, while Smart Glasses have the higher median round at $14.5 million and a much stronger strategic-investor presence.

The toughest funding transition appears surprisingly early. We found 50 Seed financings but only 13 Series A rounds, while the median check jumps from $2 million at Seed to $21.5 million at Series A.

Large checks usually arrive late in a company's development. Businesses raising more than $50 million have a median age of about seven years, compared with roughly 3.5 years for companies raising less than $5 million, although capital-intensive exceptions such as XPANCEO can reach huge rounds much earlier.

China produces more financing events than any other individual country, but US companies absorb far more money. Asia-Pacific represents 47.7% of deals and 28.5% of known capital; North America represents 30.3% of deals and 54.5% of the capital.

Monthly funding headlines are especially easy to misread in this market. October 2025 reached at least $902 million because Oura supplied roughly 99.8% of the month's known capital, while March 2026 was dominated by WHOOP and July 2025 by XPANCEO.

The broader pattern is a funding market with plenty of small entry points and very few scale winners. Investors will finance new wearable ideas, but the serious money is going to mature companies that can turn a device into a health platform, computing interface, global consumer brand or strategically valuable hardware ecosystem.

## Is wearable technology actually a well-funded market right now?

Wearable technology funding is strong at the top today, while the average startup still raises a fairly modest round.

Across the period we studied, 109 financings generated at least $3.368 billion of known equity capital. That sounds like a large market until we compare the average round with the median. The average disclosed financing is $36.6 million; the median is only $8.92 million.

A few giant transactions create most of that gap. Deals above $50 million represent just 11.9% of financings yet account for 75.7% of known capital. Take those deals away and the market shrinks from at least $3.37 billion to about $820 million.

That gives us a much clearer picture of wearable funding today. Investors are willing to put enormous sums behind a small group of companies they already believe can become major health, computing or consumer platforms. Most of the rest of the market operates at a completely different scale.

## How many wearable startups are investors really backing?

The wearable technology funding market we mapped is only 87 companies deep, and most of those companies appear once.

There are 109 financing events because some businesses raised several times during the period. Seventy-two of the 87 companies, or 82.8%, have only one qualifying financing. Ten companies raised twice and five appear three or more times.

The companies that manage to come back for more money become disproportionately important. Just 15 repeat fundraisers account for roughly 59.7% of all known capital.

So 109 deal announcements can make this market feel broader than it really is. Underneath them, we have a large group of companies that secured one visible check and a much smaller group that kept returning to investors.

That second group is where most of the serious funding eventually ends up.

## Is most wearable funding going to just a handful of companies?

Wearable technology funding is heavily concentrated, with five companies absorbing about two-thirds of all known capital.

Oura sits far ahead of everyone else with at least $1.1 billion raised during our window. WHOOP follows with $575 million, while XPANCEO contributes $250 million. Even Realities and XREAL take the top five to 66.5% of all known funding.

The concentration keeps climbing quickly after that. The top 10 companies account for 81.6%.

Meanwhile, 49.4% of companies never get beyond $5 million of visible cumulative funding, and only 11.5% cross $50 million.

That split tells us more about the market than the total amount raised. Plenty of wearable startups get funded. Very few get funded at scale.

## What does a normal wearable startup actually raise?

A normal wearable technology financing is around $9 million, and most rounds stay below $20 million.

Thirty-one deals came in below $5 million and another 34 landed between $5 million and $20 million. Together, those two buckets make up almost 60% of all financing events.

Their contribution to the dollar total is much smaller. The 31 sub-$5 million deals produced only $46.7 million of known capital. At the other extreme, six $100 million-plus transactions generated at least $2.1 billion.

For a founder, that difference is useful. A nine-figure wearable round belongs to a tiny upper layer of the market. Seed and early institutional checks in the low single-digit or low double-digit millions remain far closer to everyday reality.

There is also a meaningful middle between the two extremes. Thirteen financings landed between $20 million and $50 million, which shows that companies can reach serious institutional scale without becoming Oura or WHOOP. It simply happens much less often than the headlines suggest.

## Which wearable categories are getting real money today?

Wearable technology funding today is concentrated in two serious categories: Smart Wearables and Smart Glasses.

Smart Wearables account for 59 deals across 52 companies and at least $1.848 billion. The category covers a wide spread of health, sensing and ring businesses, with Oura creating a huge amount of the dollar weight at the top.

Smart Glasses are smaller by deal count but unusually deep for a hardware category. Twenty companies generated 33 financings and at least $824 million. Their median disclosed round is $14.5 million, almost three times the $5 million median for Smart Wearables.

The money is also less dependent on a single Smart Glasses company. Even Realities' $150 million round is the category's largest deal and represents 18.2% of its known funding. Oura alone accounts for almost half of Smart Wearables capital.

The remaining categories are much thinner. Connected Hearables have real startup activity but little scale capital. Smartwatches and XR Headsets remain small in this financing universe. Fitness Bands look huge in dollars because the entire category is WHOOP.

## Why are smart glasses attracting so much serious capital right now?

Smart Glasses funding is attracting strategic money because these startups depend heavily on optics, chips, batteries, manufacturing and distribution.

Twelve of the 33 Smart Glasses rounds involve a strategic investor. That is roughly 36% of the category and far above the 18.3% rate across wearable technology overall.

The individual investors make the pattern easy to see. Even Realities' 2026 financing involved Meituan and Tencent. The company has put a lot of its technical effort into an optical system where the chip, optics and waveguide have to work together.

XREAL has also raised from companies tied directly to the hardware supply chain. Rokid went even further in 2026, bringing in an optical-lens manufacturer, a battery supplier and a media company through separate strategic investments.

These companies need more than money. A smart-glasses startup has to solve miniature displays, prescription compatibility, heat, battery life, manufacturing yield, components, software and distribution while still producing something people are comfortable wearing on their face.

That is why corporate and industrial investors show up here so often. They can bring capabilities a conventional financial investor cannot provide by itself.

## How old are wearable startups when investors start writing big checks?

Large wearable technology rounds usually arrive after years of product development, with companies raising more than $50 million reaching a median age of roughly seven years.

The age progression across stages is quite clean despite some messy funding labels. The median Seed company is two years old when it raises. Series A moves to four years and Series B to five. Series C companies reach a median 9.5 years, while Series D+ companies sit at 11 years.

Compare that with the smallest financings: companies raising under $5 million have a median age of roughly 3.5 years.

Oura and WHOOP fit the mature end of this pattern. Both had been operating for more than a decade by the time their largest financings appeared in our window. XREAL was founded in 2017 and had already spent years developing and selling AR hardware before reaching its later-stage rounds.

XPANCEO is the obvious exception. Its $250 million Series A arrived only a few years after the company was founded. The underlying project also has unusually heavy technical requirements, spanning optics, photonics, materials science, biosensing and smart-contact-lens development.

The stage name alone therefore tells us less than the work being financed. Investors can write an enormous early-stage check when the technical program itself requires enormous amounts of capital.

## Where do wearable startups get stuck between Seed and scale?

The wearable technology funding ladder narrows most sharply between Seed and Series A.

We found 50 Seed financings and only 13 Series A rounds, a ratio of 3.85 Seed deals for every Series A. After that, the gaps become much smaller: 13 A rounds become nine B rounds, nine B rounds become six C rounds, and six C rounds become five D+ transactions.

The check size changes at exactly the same point. Median Seed financing is $2 million. At Series A, it jumps to $21.5 million.

That is a huge reset in what investors expect from the company. A startup can raise a few million dollars around a strong technical idea, prototype or early product. Raising twenty-plus million dollars usually requires a much more convincing case around product readiness, manufacturing, commercial demand or defensible technology.

The toughest part of the wearable funding journey therefore appears surprisingly early. Companies that clear the Series A hurdle encounter a much less brutal numerical drop-off later in the ladder.

## Who gets the money today: new wearable startups or companies that have already raised?

Wearable technology funding today overwhelmingly goes to companies that already have a financing history.

Only 24 of the 109 transactions qualify as a company's first financing in our research window. Those first rounds represent 22% of deals but just $114.5 million of known capital, around 3.4% of the total.

Their median disclosed round is $1.63 million. Follow-on rounds reach a $12 million median.

The difference becomes even clearer in aggregate: follow-ons absorb roughly 96.6% of known capital.

We are therefore seeing a market where new companies continue to enter at the bottom, but investors put serious money behind businesses that have already survived at least one previous fundraising cycle.

For founders, the funding story changes dramatically after that first proof point. Getting funded once is relatively common in this dataset. Building enough momentum to keep coming back for larger checks is where the market starts separating companies very quickly.

## Who actually keeps funding wearable startups?

Wearable technology funding has surprisingly few repeat lead investors, while corporate backers show up selectively around companies with obvious strategic value.

We could identify a defensible lead investor in 70 financings. Across those deals, only three named leads appear more than once: Fidelity Management & Research, Ant Group and Khosla Ventures.

Fidelity's two appearances are both tied to Oura and therefore carry an enormous dollar value. Ant Group appears twice across Smart Wearables Series A/B financings. Khosla Ventures repeats at Seed. Beyond those names, lead investors are remarkably scattered.

Corporate investors tell a different story. Twenty of the 109 financings include at least one strategic backer, and their median round is $14.58 million compared with $6.9 million for rounds without one.

The latest deals make that easy to understand. Dexcom invested directly in Oura alongside a commercial partnership. Abbott participated in WHOOP's 2026 round. Qualcomm Ventures joined Ultrahuman's recent financing. Smart-glasses companies repeatedly attract component, manufacturing and technology partners.

Corporate money remains a minority of overall deal activity, but it becomes much more visible once a wearable company overlaps with an established healthcare, semiconductor, optical or consumer-electronics business.

## Is the US or China leading wearable funding right now?

Wearable technology funding is broader in China right now, while US companies attract much more capital per deal.

China generated 39 financings in our dataset, more than any other individual country. Those deals account for at least $744 million.

The United States produced 32 transactions and at least $1.833 billion.

Oura and WHOOP create a large part of that dollar gap. China's strength is broader across Smart Glasses, with companies such as XREAL, Rokid, Even Realities and RayNeo raising repeatedly.

The regional numbers show the same split. Asia-Pacific accounts for 47.7% of deals but 28.5% of known capital. North America produces 30.3% of deals and 54.5% of the money.

Europe sits further down the scale, with 23 transactions and roughly $324 million. It has meaningful company activity, although its rounds are much smaller on average.

So the US currently looks like the place where a few mature wearable companies can raise extraordinary sums. China looks like the deeper hardware startup market, particularly in glasses.

## Are big wearable funding quarters actually broad booms?

Wearable technology funding spikes are usually one-company events rather than broad rushes of investors into the whole market.

Deal activity is fairly steady. Across the 32 completed months from January 2024 through August 2026, the market averages 3.34 financings per month and has a median of three. Even the busiest month reaches only eight deals.

Funding dollars move far more violently.

October 2025 reached at least $902 million, with Oura supplying roughly 99.8% of the month's known capital. In March 2026, WHOOP accounted for 83.1% of the $691.6 million total. In July 2025, XPANCEO generated 88.6% of that month's capital.

The highest deal-count month is only 2.7 times the median month. The biggest capital month is almost 27 times the median.

That makes headlines such as "wearable funding surged this quarter" dangerous without looking underneath. A real broad upswing would show up across the number of companies raising, typical round sizes and the amount of capital outside the biggest transactions. One giant check can move the headline total by hundreds of millions of dollars without changing conditions for everyone else.

## Which wearable categories still fail to produce scale-ups?

Connected Hearables, Smartwatches and XR Headsets all struggle to produce a visible scale-up layer in our wearable funding data.

Connected Hearables are the clearest case. We found 10 financings across eight companies, yet none is classified as Series B or later. There are companies being formed and funded, but the later financing ladder simply never appears during our window.

Smartwatches look even smaller as an independent venture category. Three companies produce three deals and only $13.3 million of disclosed capital.

XR Headsets have three financings worth $19.5 million in total. That is too small a sample to make sweeping claims about the whole XR industry, especially because category boundaries can move businesses into Smart Glasses or other markets. It does show that independent headset startups barely register in this particular financing population.

Fitness Bands sit at the opposite extreme. The category appears enormous at $575 million, but every dollar comes from one WHOOP transaction.

Looking at what is missing prevents us from treating "wearables" as one homogeneous venture market. Several product categories have startup activity without developing anything close to the financing depth we see in Smart Wearables and Smart Glasses.

## Are the biggest wearable companies still just selling devices?

The biggest wearable technology companies are increasingly using the device as the front door to a broader health or computing platform.

Oura's largest financing was tied to global expansion and health innovation. Its Dexcom partnership also connected ring data with glucose information, pushing the product further into continuous personal-health interpretation.

WHOOP has been moving in the same direction. Its 2026 financing was built around a personalized-health platform, with investors including Abbott and Mayo Clinic alongside financial institutions and athletes.

Ultrahuman is one of the freshest examples in the dataset. Its September 2026 financing package totaled $70 million; we record $65 million because the remaining $5 million was debt. The company described the new capital around a broader health-intelligence and human-computer-interface strategy rather than simply selling more smart rings.

The computing side shows a similar expansion. XPANCEO wants its smart contact lens to combine display, sensing and vision functions. XREAL has been building an AR hardware-and-software stack around spatial computing. Even Realities is developing its own integrated optics rather than assembling a generic display product.

Large wearable investors these days are often funding an ongoing relationship with the user: health data, software, AI, interfaces and services wrapped around a physical device. That gives the biggest companies far more room to grow than a one-off hardware sale.

## What should we actually take away from wearable technology funding?

Wearable technology funding today is broad at the small-round end and brutally concentrated at the top.

Dozens of companies can raise a few million dollars. A much smaller group gets access to the tens or hundreds of millions needed to build global consumer brands, complex hardware supply chains or deeper health and computing platforms.

The hardest jump appears early. Seed activity is plentiful, then the market gets much more demanding at Series A. Companies that survive that jump have a much more realistic path toward later institutional capital.

Smart Wearables and Smart Glasses are currently the two categories with enough company activity and money to feel like genuine venture markets. Smart Wearables produce the biggest mature health businesses. Smart Glasses have developed a broader hardware cluster where strategic investors are unusually involved.

The money also has a strong memory. Investors keep directing the overwhelming majority of capital toward companies that have already raised, while first financings remain small.

Finally, headline funding totals need to be treated carefully. As we saw above, one Oura, WHOOP or XPANCEO financing can transform an entire month's numbers. The underlying wearable market moves much more slowly: a few deals each month, many modest checks, a difficult Seed-to-Series-A jump and a very small group of companies reaching serious scale.

## OUR METHODOLOGY

We studied private wearable-technology financings announced from January 1, 2024 through September 21, 2026. The goal was to measure how fundraising in the market actually works rather than reproduce the output of a single funding database.

Wearable technology had to represent the company's core product or an overwhelming part of the business at the time of financing. We included qualifying private-company equity financings and isolated the equity portion when a transaction mixed equity with debt or other forms of capital. Pure debt, loans and grants were excluded from deal values.

Each genuinely distinct capital injection was recorded separately, while duplicate reports of the same financing were consolidated. Extensions, additional closes and tranches were checked individually. For example, dotLumen's July 2024 opening and January 2025 closing were treated as the same €5 million financing, while Oura's $75 million Dexcom investment and subsequent $125 million close were kept as separate injections within its $200 million Series D.

The final analytical population contains 109 financing events across 87 companies. Seventeen financings have no usable publicly disclosed amount, 22 amounts involve approximate currency conversions and seven are lower bounds, so aggregate figures are reported as minimum-known capital where necessary. September 2026 is a partial month; monthly averages and medians therefore use the 32 completed months through August 2026.

We recorded dates, stages, lead investors, strategic investors, company history and deal values only when public evidence supported them. We did not infer stages from round sizes, assume that a prominent participant was the lead investor, or manufacture exact dollar values from disclosures that only supported an approximation or minimum figure.

Our research relied on company and investor announcements, regulatory disclosures, established funding databases and reputable financial or technology media. We also ran a separate quality-control pass for missed financings, boundary dates, company eligibility, financing structures, duplicate reports and unusual multi-close transactions.
