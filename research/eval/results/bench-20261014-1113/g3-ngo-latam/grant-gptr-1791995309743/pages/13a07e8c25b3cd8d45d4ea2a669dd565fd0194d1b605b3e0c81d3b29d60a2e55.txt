# Rural Health Transformation Program funding for maternal care, state by state

By Ouma Health Checked September 30, 2026 Updated September 30, 2026

States receive an average of about $200 million a year from the Rural Health Transformation Program through 2030. We read 21 state plans against the states' own documents. Eleven put a dollar figure on maternal care. Nine name it, or have funded maternal projects, without a maternal line item. One names it only as an outcome. The table below shows what each state funded, what is open, and where a hospital or clinic can still get in.

The program

## The program in five facts

01 $50 billion over five years. $10 billion a year, fiscal years 2026 through 2030. CMS announced awards to all 50 states on December 29, 2025. Year-one awards run from $147.3 million (New Jersey) to $281.3 million (Texas), and the average is $200 million. Half of the money is split equally, about $100 million a state. The other half is allocated on rural population, facility mix, uncompensated care and the policy commitments each state made in its application.

02 There are two deadlines, and the first is October 30. A state has to commit its year-one money, meaning sign the contracts and subawards, by October 30, 2026, and spend it by September 30, 2027. CMS doesn't count money that is earmarked but not paid out. Money a state fails to spend goes back to CMS and is redistributed to other states.

03 Nobody applies to CMS except the state. Each state runs its own subawards, and the routes differ: competitive grant rounds open to rural providers, direct contracts with a university or a hospital association, or regional hubs that pass most of their award down to local projects. A hospital, clinic or birth center applies to its state, or to its regional hub. A telehealth company doesn't apply at all; it is named as a partner in someone else's application.

04 The law never mentions pregnancy. The statute lists what a state can fund: prevention and chronic disease, payments to providers, consumer technology, technology training, workforce, health IT, right-sizing service lines, behavioral health, care models, and other uses CMS approves. CMS's funding notice turned that last item into two more categories, capital and regional partnerships, and added the caps: provider payments at no more than 15% of a state's award, capital at no more than 20%, and no new construction. The same notice names "prenatal care" in the program's first strategic goal, and its list of example initiatives is where maternal care shows up in detail: maternity care shortage areas, access to maternal-fetal medicine providers, perinatal quality collaboratives. Whether the money reaches obstetrics depends on what each state wrote into its plan and on who applied.

05 Year two is being decided now. CMS has committed to determining each state's fiscal 2027 amount by October 31, 2026, the day year two's budget period begins. Oklahoma tells applicants that CMS has asked states to plan for a similar funding level. Several states have said when their next window opens, and those dates are in the table.

## Where maternal care is funded, and where it isn't

Twenty-one states, read from each state's own plan, budget narrative, funding summary, notices of funding opportunity and award announcements, not from the one-page abstracts CMS publishes. The abstracts miss most of the maternal content. Georgia's mentions newborn screening, interhospital transport and nutrition services for pregnant women and nothing on obstetric care, and Texas's doesn't mention maternal care at all, yet Georgia funds two maternal initiatives and Texas names maternal health as an early focus of its telehealth program. ## The coverage gap the money is meant to close

Using the money

## How a rural hospital, clinic or birth center uses this money for obstetric coverage

The money pays for programs and services, not buildings. New construction is barred, capital is capped at 20% of a state's award, and payments to providers for items or services are capped at 15%. That shape suits a maternal telehealth line: consult coverage, ultrasound interpretation, remote monitoring, training and the technology to run them fit the categories for consumer technology, technology training, health IT, service-line design and regional partnerships, without touching the provider-payment cap. Two cautions. The program doesn't pay for services insurance already covers (CMS bars it in every state, and Colorado's FAQ spells it out for billable telehealth), so the budget line should cover the parts a claim doesn't: availability, the platform, training, coordination. And every state runs its own rules, so read the state's notice before you write the budget.

Without labor and delivery
A hospital doesn't need a labor and delivery unit to have an obstetric problem. A pregnant patient in distress often goes to the nearest emergency department, whatever services it offers. Alabama's maternal round didn't require existing OB services, and Georgia put stocked obstetric emergency carts in rural hospitals that don't deliver. Consult coverage for that emergency department is a legitimate line in the same budget.

Two ways in
There are two ways in. The first is to apply in your state's round with the maternal work written into your own project: name the clinical partner, put a budget line against consults and reads, and describe how the service continues after the grant (CMS asks every state to show sustainability, and a service that bills Medicaid after year one answers that). The second is to attach to someone else's award. New Mexico's and South Dakota's regional hubs must spend most of their money on local projects. Iowa's hub and network winners, due to be named around October 16, will need the coverage they promised. California's Accelerator Partners form networks that include birth centers, and Texas's December telehealth round is open only to networks of rural providers. If your state's rounds have closed, the network route is usually still open.

Timing
Timing matters more than usual. Year-one money has to be committed by October 30, 2026 and spent by September 30, 2027. A program that can start within weeks is worth more to a state agency than one that needs a building.

Frequently asked

## Questions we get about this program

Can the money pay for tele-MFM? &rarr;

Can a telehealth company apply for Rural Health Transformation funds? No. States receive the awards and subaward them to rural providers and organizations under their own rules. A telehealth practice takes part as a named partner or subcontractor in a hospital's, clinic's or hub's application. Arkansas, Florida, Maryland and Oregon say this in their guidance.

Can the money pay for tele-MFM consults or ultrasound reads? It depends on the state's approved plan and its subaward terms. Oklahoma funds a tele-MFM network directly. Iowa's maternal hub-and-spoke program lists maternal-fetal medicine care by telemedicine and teleultrasound in scope. Pennsylvania's plan links remote monitoring to tele-MFM consults, and two Pennsylvania hospitals used first-round money on ultrasound machines for telehealth and in-person MFM care. Texas's telehealth initiative names maternal care. Other states fund telehealth generally and let the applicant define the service. The program doesn't pay for services insurance already covers, so the grant goes to the parts a claim doesn't.

Can a hospital without labor and delivery use this money for obstetric coverage? In several states, yes. Alabama's maternal round didn't require existing OB services, and Georgia equipped rural hospitals that don't deliver with obstetric emergency carts. A pregnant patient in distress often goes to the nearest emergency department, so consult coverage for that department belongs in the budget.

My state's CMS abstract doesn't mention maternal health. Does that settle it? No. The CMS abstracts are one page. Georgia's mentions newborn screening, interhospital transport and nutrition services for pregnant women and nothing on obstetric care, and Georgia funds two maternal initiatives. Texas's doesn't mention maternal care, and its plan names maternal health as an early focus of its telehealth initiative. Read the state's project narrative and its funding page.

When is the next round? CMS determines each state's fiscal 2027 amount by October 31, 2026. Texas's telehealth round, which names maternal care, takes applications in December 2026. Oklahoma says year-two opportunities begin opening in January 2027, Alabama that applications reopen for year two, and Pennsylvania lists a maternal remote monitoring round as upcoming. Most other states haven't published year-two dates.

What happens to money a state doesn't spend? It goes back to CMS, which redistributes unspent funds to other states. The first redistribution is calculated by March 31, 2028. CMS doesn't count money that is earmarked but not paid out as spent.
