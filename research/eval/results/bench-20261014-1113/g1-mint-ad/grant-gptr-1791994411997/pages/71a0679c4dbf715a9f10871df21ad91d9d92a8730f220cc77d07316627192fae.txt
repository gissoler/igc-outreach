## Introduction

More than 7 million Americans are estimated to currently live with Alzheimer’s disease, and many others live with an Alzheimer’s disease-related dementia. This number is expected to rise as the number of older adults in America continues to grow. The impacts are substantial: people living with Alzheimer’s and related dementias experience significant impacts on their quality of life, and there can be considerable strain on their families and loved ones who provide care. The economic costs of dementia are also substantial, with a recent report estimating that dementia will cost Americans $818 billion in 2026 . In short, there’s an urgent need to address Alzheimer’s and related dementias. The National Institutes of Health (NIH) is meeting that urgency with action.

### NIH leads the nation’s dementia research strategy
NIH drives the nation’s research to better understand the complex and varied causes of Alzheimer’s and related dementias, identify early signs of disease, develop effective interventions to prevent or delay disease progression, and improve care and support for those living with dementia as well as their care partners. NIH investments also include vital research on understanding the factors that drive risk and resilience to ultimately reduce the prevalence of dementia.
This work is carried out across NIH, with the National Institute on Aging (NIA) and National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NINDS) leading these efforts. The National Plan to Address Alzheimer’s Disease, which emerged from the National Alzheimer’s Project Act, spurred a substantial increase in federal dementia research efforts. While Alzheimer’s is the most common dementia diagnosis, related dementias — such as frontotemporal dementias (FTD), Lewy body dementias (LBD), vascular dementia, and mixed or multiple etiology dementia — share many cognitive symptoms and brain changes, which can make differential diagnosis challenging. In addition, the majority of older adults with dementia have more than one pathology or form of dementia at the same time, called multiple etiology or mixed dementia. NIH also supports research on vascular contributions to cognitive impairment and dementia (VCID), a broader scientific framework that includes vascular dementia and other ways vascular factors may affect cognitive decline and dementia. Thanks to sustained federal investments in dementia research, NIH has made significant progress in understanding, diagnosing, preventing, and treating Alzheimer’s and related dementias, as well as in advancing evidence-based models of dementia care and caregiving.

In the pages that follow, you will find descriptions of scientific and clinical advances in dementia research in 2025, spanning the entirety of the research enterprise from basic to translational to clinical efforts. Throughout this report, you will also see advances that are improving the understanding of sex-based differences in Alzheimer's disease and related dementias, including investigations into how sex influences mechanisms of risk, resilience, disease heterogeneity, and treatment response. Advances in these areas of research are signified by the icon on the right.
These advancements move us closer to the development of precision medicine approaches tailored for individuals. Importantly, these advances are made possible by the individuals living with dementia and their loved ones who volunteer their time and energy to participate in dementia research — for this, NIH is deeply appreciative.
NIH-funded research has driven remarkable progress in understanding and addressing Alzheimer’s and related dementias. With sustained investment and the advent and expanding capabilities of next-generation research technologies and approaches, including powerful new tools made possible by artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML), NIH will continue to build on this progress to accelerate discovery and drive real-world impacts for all Americans living with dementia and their families.

## Preventing and treating Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias
Remarkable progress in research on dementia prevention and treatment has been made in the past several years, made possible in part by foundational research funded by NIH. However, a significant need remains for new interventions and therapeutics to address the complex nature of Alzheimer’s and related dementias, as well as optimization and enhancement of existing therapeutics to make them safer, more accessible, and more effective.

### Expanding the therapeutic pipeline

Currently, there are two Food and Drug Administration-approved drugs, lecanemab (Leqembi) and donanemab (Kisunla), for treating early Alzheimer’s. NIH continues to fund clinical trials aimed at understanding the effectiveness of these drugs in treating different stages of Alzheimer’s, including in people at risk for but not diagnosed with Alzheimer’s, both alone and in combination with other drugs. NIH has also funded research examining the impact of these drugs as they begin to be used in clinical settings to treat Alzheimer’s, including the consideration of treatment access barriers, such as high costs and the logistics for in-person infusions at specialized centers. For example, NIH-supported research indicates there are disparities in the use, or uptake, of lecanemab among Medicare beneficiaries : women, rural residents, ethnic minorities, and lower income individuals were less likely to be using lecanemab despite meeting initial Medicare coverage requirements for the drug. Separately, simulations using data from a key phase 3 trial of lecanemab suggest that the drug is more likely to be clinically effective in males than in females . NIH continues to support research examining how use of approved drugs in clinical and other everyday settings impacts individuals and the broader American public.

#### New drug candidates
NIH leads significant efforts to expand the therapeutic pipeline to meet the needs of all individuals who may be at risk of developing Alzheimer’s and related dementias.
To date, few clinical trials have tested drug candidates in people with two copies of the APOE4 gene variant, the strongest genetic risk factor for Alzheimer’s. An NIH-funded phase 3 trial of the oral drug candidate ALZ-801 in 325 people with two copies of APOE4 and either mild cognitive impairment or early Alzheimer’s did not show clinical efficacy of the drug candidate. However, a subgroup analysis of participants with mild cognitive impairment, as opposed to early Alzheimer’s, found that ALZ-801 slowed both brain atrophy and cognitive decline , as measured by two common cognitive tests. These findings will inform the design of future clinical research.
Importantly, NIH is supporting multiple potential therapeutic targets for Alzheimer’s and related dementias. For example, a recently completed NIH-funded phase 2 trial of the small molecule CT1812 in people with dementia with Lewy bodies reported a favorable safety profile. In addition, CT1812 is also being evaluated in an ongoing phase 2 trial involving 540 participants with early Alzheimer’s. Notably, NIH supported the discovery, preclinical, and clinical development of CT1812, including earlier first-in-human trials.
Likewise, findings from a phase 2 clinical trial of the drug candidate LM11A-31, developed with NIH support, suggest that LM11A-31, which targets the p75 neurotrophin receptor and supports neuronal survival, is safe and well-tolerated in people with early Alzheimer’s and could potentially mitigate some of the brain damage caused by the condition. These results support further research to assess the drug candidate’s long-term safety and effectiveness in treating early Alzheimer’s. The drug candidate is also being examined as a potential treatment for other neurodegenerative conditions.
In addition to funding clinical trials of existing drug candidates, NIH works to identify the next generation of drug candidates and strategically de-risk dementia drug development. NIH’s translational research funding initiatives, including the Alzheimer’s Drug Development Program (ADDP) , are accelerating the development of new drug candidates aimed at a broad range of disease processes and targets, including inflammation, growth factors, hormones, and proteins implicated in synaptic function, and brain metabolism. Since their launch, ADDP and related drug development programs have advanced 25 new drug candidates to human trials: 17 in phase 1 trials, 7 in phase 2 trials, and 1 in a phase 3 trial.
In addition to the 25 drug candidates that have advanced to clinical trials, another 53 drug candidates supported by ADDP and related programs are in preclinical development. As one example, the drug candidate Hu6, targeting the pituitary hormone FSH (follicle stimulating hormone), is showing promise for the treatment of Alzheimer’s as well as obesity and osteoporosis .

#### Drug repurposing
NIH also supports drug repurposing efforts, in which drugs already FDA-approved for conditions other than dementia are tested for efficacy in preventing or treating Alzheimer’s and related dementias. Drug repurposing offers a promising avenue to identify dementia therapeutics faster and at lower cost than traditional drug development. Many of these efforts leverage innovative data analysis tools to help identify promising drug candidates and targets. For example, an NIH-funded study used a computational drug repurposing algorithm along with other biological data analytical tools to predict which combinations of FDA-approved drugs may be useful for the treatment of Alzheimer’s. The study identified a combination therapy involving two FDA-approved cancer drugs, letrozole and irinotecan, and showed that this combination improved memory and reduced Alzheimer’s pathology in mouse models.
Another NIH-funded study of the drug lemborexant, approved for treating insomnia, showed that it reduced markers of neurodegeneration in a mouse model of Alzheimer’s. NIH is now supporting a clinical trial that is testing lemborexant’s efficacy for the treatment of Alzheimer’s. Another study examining subcutaneous administration of GM-CSF — a drug used to stimulate the immune system after bone marrow transplants or chemotherapy — over three weeks was reported to halt neurodegeneration , as measured via biomarkers, in both a rodent model of Alzheimer’s and in people with Alzheimer’s. These results complement the findings of an earlier short-term clinical trial of the drug candidate, funded in part by NIH, in which treatment with GM-CSF was found to improve scores on a measure of cognition in people with Alzheimer’s.

### Behavioral, lifestyle, and other interventions

NIH also supports clinical trials of a broad range of non-pharmacological approaches (e.g., behavioral and lifestyle changes) for preventing and treating dementia. Results from the U.S. POINTER trial demonstrated that a multidomain lifestyle intervention – including exercise, diet, cognitive and social engagement, and health monitoring – improved global cognition in high-risk older adults over two years. These U.S. POINTER results complement findings from a recent NIH-funded trial which indicated that personalized health coaching improved cognition and reduced dementia risk in older adults with at least two modifiable risk factors for dementia (e.g., low physical activity, hypertension, diabetes, smoking).
While the main U.S. POINTER trial was not NIH-funded, NIH is funding four ancillary studies focusing on neurovascular function, sleep, neuroimaging, and the microbiome to uncover the biological mechanisms behind these benefits. Neuroimaging results indicate that the structured intervention may enhance cognitive resilience in people with low hippocampal volume or elevated brain tau. Additionally, analysis of brain imaging data from U.S. POINTER participants found that the POINTER lifestyle intervention reduced damage to tiny blood vessels in the brains of individuals under age 70 but not in those 70 or older. Early findings also suggest that improvements in neurovascular function and sleep quality may contribute to cognitive gains.
Additionally, findings from an earlier NIH-funded clinical study suggested that a specific form of cognitive training known as speed-of-processing training improved cognition in older adults at risk of cognitive decline. Using Medicare claims data, NIH-funded researchers found that older adults who had completed this training along with booster sessions had a 25% lower risk of being diagnosed with Alzheimer’s and other dementias over a 20-year follow-up period .

## Accelerating development of tools for dementia diagnoses and cognitive assessment

NIH-funded research has revolutionized how doctors and researchers can detect Alzheimer’s and related dementias in people at different stages of disease. In the past, diagnosis relied mostly on observing symptoms. Today, researchers can also measure biological signs of disease using tools such as brain scans and tests that assess levels of proteins present in blood and spinal fluid samples. New AI-driven clinical decision tools and scalable cognitive tests are improving the measurement of dementia risk, as well as cognitive and functional symptoms. Moreover, these advances provide the basis for earlier detection of Alzheimer’s and related dementias and thereby provide opportunities for earlier interventions and more efficient clinical trial screening. However, important gaps remain, especially for dementias other than Alzheimer’s, to detect these diseases early, before symptoms appear. NIH-funded researchers continue to work toward diagnostic tools that are more sensitive, more accessible, and capable of detecting disease as early as possible.

### Blood and spinal fluid biomarkers

Researchers made significant progress over the past year in developing and refining blood and spinal fluid biomarkers that can diagnose and track the progression of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias. A newly identified blood biomarker, called MTBR-tau243, was found to reflect the amount of tau tangle pathology , a defining hallmark of Alzheimer's, with levels rising progressively from the mild cognitive impairment stage through more severe dementia. Notably, MTBR-tau243 outperformed other plasma tau biomarkers in tracking disease severity. These findings suggest that this biomarker could be a promising tool for both diagnosis and monitoring the effectiveness of tau-targeting treatments. A separate large study found that a ratio of two synaptic proteins measured in cerebrospinal fluid may be useful as a biomarker for predicting the transition from normal cognition to mild cognitive impairment or dementia up to 15 years in advance of symptom appearance.

In addition, the Women’s Health Initiative Memory Study, funded by NIH, showed that a higher baseline p-tau217 in the blood of Black and White women with normal cognition was associated with development of dementia during up to 25 years of study follow-up. These findings suggest that elevated p-tau217 levels can predict dementia risk in women potentially decades before symptoms begin , a lead time that could open a meaningful window for early intervention.
Other NIH-funded research findings may help improve the accessibility of biomarker testing by demonstrating that Alzheimer's-related proteins can be reliably detected from blood spots collected from a simple finger prick rather than a traditional blood draw. Importantly, the results obtained from dried blood spots closely match those from standard blood samples. This research represents a potentially important step toward making biomarker testing less invasive and more broadly available in a wider range of settings.
Two additional NIH-funded studies focused on improving biomarker tools for Lewy body-related diseases, which include Parkinson’s disease and two forms of dementia, Parkinson's disease dementia and dementia with Lewy bodies. Blood and spinal fluid tests for different forms of the alpha-synuclein protein, used together with Alzheimer's biomarker profiles, showed promise in distinguishing between these conditions and predicting how quickly symptoms may worsen . These findings could help meaningfully improve diagnosis and care planning for these often-misdiagnosed diseases.
Investigators are also exploring how well biomarker tests perform across different populations. In a preclinical Alzheimer's trial, NIH-funded researchers found that plasma biomarker eligibility rates differed by participant race and ethnicity , with Black, Asian, and Hispanic individuals less likely to be deemed trial-eligible based on blood biomarker screening. Another study comparing biomarker associations in older adults in India and the United States found that while high levels of the neurodegeneration markers GFAP and NfL were strongly associated with dementia risk in both countries, other biomarkers behaved differently across populations — including the amyloid ratio Aβ42/Aβ40, which was linked to worse cognitive functioning in American participants but better cognitive functioning in Indian participants. Together, these findings underscore the need to validate biomarker tools across different populations.

### Tools to help predict the timing of dementia onset
A promising frontier in Alzheimer's diagnostics is the development of tools that can predict not just whether a person will develop dementia, but when. NIH-funded researchers developed a "clock model" blood test using a marker based on phosphorylated tau protein (p-tau217) that can both assess a person's risk of Alzheimer's and estimate when they are likely to begin experiencing symptoms, with an accuracy of three to four years.
Complementing these blood-based findings, researchers also developed and validated the Florey Dementia Index , a tool that uses just two pieces of readily available information — a person's age and their score on a standard cognition test — to predict the age at which they are likely to develop mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer's. Because it requires no blood draw or imaging, the Florey Dementia Index could serve as a practical clinical tool to identify people at risk early, inform care planning, and in the future help prioritize patients for further screening for disease-modifying treatments.
Together, these advances reflect a shift in the field toward tools that enable earlier detection and potentially forecast dementia trajectory, giving at-risk individuals, caregivers, and clinicians a clearer picture of what may lie ahead and providing more time to act.

### Brain imaging

Brain imaging technologies are giving researchers increasingly powerful tools to detect the earliest biological signatures of Alzheimer's and related dementias, often years before clinical symptoms emerge. Recent NIH-funded studies highlight the growing potential of MRI- and PET-based biomarkers to improve early diagnosis, risk identification, and disease monitoring across broad populations.
For example, NIH-funded investigators developed DunedinPACNI , a novel MRI-derived measure of the pace of biological aging. Researchers found that individuals with the highest DunedinPACNI scores at baseline were approximately 1.5 times more likely to develop mild cognitive impairment or dementia compared to those with average scores. Validated across multiple large international study groups, DunedinPACNI also predicted accelerated brain shrinkage, physical frailty, and mortality, positioning it as a next-generation biomarker for evaluating aging-related health outcomes more broadly.
VCID encompasses all types of cerebrovascular and cardiovascular disease-related cognitive decline. Cerebrovascular disease is exceedingly common in older adults diagnosed with Alzheimer's and often co-occurs with related dementias. NIH-funded researchers are developing effective new vascular biomarkers to eventually be used in combination with other dementia-specific biomarkers (e.g., amyloid, tau, synuclein, TDP-43) to inform interventions to slow disease progression and deliver personalized dementia treatments. For example, NIH-funded researchers developed a brain imaging-based tool using PET and MRI to track neurovascular and metabolic changes across multiple brain regions, finding that blood flow and glucose metabolism become dysregulated years before the onset of dementia symptoms. This dysregulation follows a stage-dependent pattern, with brain regions associated with memory, cognition, and motor function showing the earliest changes. Importantly, the rate and nature of these changes varied across brain regions and differed by sex, underscoring the tool's potential to support more precise diagnosis and tracking of dementia progression.
Separately, a large-scale NIH-funded study of more than 20,000 brain MRI scans from over 10,000 participants across three studies looked at differences in perivascular spaces (PVS), the fluid-filled compartments surrounding brain blood vessels. The investigators found that having fewer and larger spaces predicted higher dementia risk and accelerated brain atrophy over time. Further, incorporating these PVS-based markers increased statistical power in simulated clinical trial screenings, suggesting they could enhance risk stratification of trial participants, reduce trial costs, and improve overall trial efficiency.
Other NIH-funded investigators used a special type of MRI to create the Axonal Density Index (ADI) , a novel measure capable of detecting early axonal degeneration, a hallmark feature of most forms of dementia. The researchers demonstrated that lower baseline ADI values predicted faster cognitive and functional decline with greater sensitivity than standard spinal fluid biomarkers and comparable accuracy to current gold-standard biomarkers, establishing ADI as a promising tool for early detection and disease monitoring.
Together, these advances reflect a rapidly expanding suite of sensitive, noninvasive brain imaging tools that align with NIH's precision medicine goals of detecting disease earlier and characterizing individual risk more accurately.

### Low-cost and scalable tools for detecting cognitive impairment in clinical settings

For many individuals and families, an early dementia diagnosis could mean faster access to care, more time to plan, and greater opportunity to benefit from emerging treatments. However, early detection of Alzheimer's and related dementias remains a persistent challenge in primary care and community health settings, where access to specialized diagnostic tools is often limited. Researchers are developing and validating a new generation of low-cost, scalable approaches that can be embedded into existing clinical workflows to improve the rate and timeliness of dementia diagnoses.
A randomized clinical trial conducted across nine federally qualified health centers in Indianapolis found that combining two scalable digital tools significantly improved early dementia detection in primary care . The Quick Dementia Rating System, a patient-reported outcome measure, was paired with the Passive Digital Marker (PDM), a machine learning algorithm that draws on existing electronic health record data. Among more than 5,300 adults ages 65 and older, clinics using the combined approach were 31% more likely to make a new dementia diagnosis and 41% more likely to initiate a diagnostic workup compared to usual care, while clinics using the PDM alone showed no significant improvement. The approach required minimal additional time and effort from primary care teams.
Likewise, the NIH-funded DetectCID Consortium evaluated the TabCAT Brain Health Assessment , a digital cognitive screening tool administered by medical assistants and integrated directly into electronic health records, across 21 primary care providers serving more than 2,700 patients. The tool was adopted by 95% of the study’s providers, who were subsequently 72% more likely to diagnose a cognitive disorder compared to prior practice. Notably, providers reported decreased stress and increased confidence around dementia diagnosis, and diagnosis rates remained elevated even after research support ended, suggesting strong potential for long-term sustainability. The study also found increased diagnosis rates among minority patient groups, pointing to the tool's potential to help reduce disparities in dementia detection.
To further accelerate progress in dementia diagnostics, NIH launched the PREPARE Challenge , an innovation competition designed to advance new methods and open datasets for early prediction of Alzheimer's and related dementias across a diverse range of data modalities. The competition sought to harness the creativity and expertise of the broader community to develop accurate AI/ML-powered tools that are generalizable to real-world clinical contexts. Winning approaches demonstrated that cognitive decline can be detected through changes in speech as well as from models trained on survey data collected from across international cohorts. Code and documentation from winning teams have been made publicly available to help advance dementia research.
These advances represent meaningful progress toward making dementia detection faster, more affordable, and more accessible across a wider range of clinical and community settings. By integrating seamlessly into existing workflows, protecting individual privacy, and making analytical tools openly available to the research community, these innovations can help lower barriers to early diagnosis.

### Other diagnostic tools
Several NIH-funded studies have also advanced the development and validation of at-home, self-administered cognitive screening tools with strong potential to expand early detection beyond the clinic. MyCog 2.0, an updated app-based cognitive assessment tool redesigned based on clinician and patient feedback, demonstrated promise in measuring both executive function and episodic memory in a community sample of 200 older adults, supporting its use as a reliable, self-administered screener specifically designed for ease of use in this population.

Another NIH-supported study found that a smartphone-based cognitive assessment platform, the Mobile Toolbox, was successfully self-administered by cognitively unimpaired older adults on their personal devices, with Toolbox cognition scores significantly associated with both standardized in-clinic cognitive testing and brain tau levels. These results suggest that the Mobile Toolbox may be able to capture early cognitive changes relevant to preclinical Alzheimer's.
Collectively, these advances reflect a growing recognition that expanding access to early dementia diagnosis requires tools that meet patients and clinicians where they are, whether at home with a smartphone or through a routine medical procedure. By reducing barriers of cost, complexity, and invasiveness, these innovations hold promise for reaching individuals who might otherwise go undiagnosed, including those in rural and other communities. Continued research to validate and implement these tools across broad populations will be essential to realizing their full potential in addressing the public health challenge of Alzheimer's and related dementias.

### SPOTLIGHT: Artificial intelligence, machine learning, and data-driven diagnostic tools

Diagnosing Alzheimer’s and related dementias is a complex endeavor. Effective diagnostic frameworks need to encompass a wide range of overlapping disease pathologies, subtle early warning signs, and significant variation across populations. This challenge has spurred researchers to develop innovative AI/ML tools that can augment clinical decision-making, improve early detection, and expand access to diagnostic capabilities across communities.
With support from NIH, researchers developed StateViewer, an innovative AI diagnostic tool using PET brain imaging and machine learning to distinguish nine distinct types of dementia-related neurodegeneration patterns, including Alzheimer’s, with up to 88% accuracy as compared to standard image analysis. Accordingly, the researchers report that use of this tool may improve diagnosis speed and give clinicians more than three times greater odds of arriving at a correct diagnosis compared to a standard-of-care workflow. Other NIH-funded research supported the development of a new multimodal computational framework that integrates data from standard neurological clinical exams to estimate amyloid beta and tau brain profiles without the need for costly imaging, offering a practical and scalable tool for pre-screening candidates for clinical trials.
By analyzing electronic health data using machine learning approaches, NIH-funded researchers have developed and validated a two-year dementia risk prediction model for American Indian and Alaska Native older adults , a population for whom no such tools had previously existed. The model achieved strong predictive accuracy and identified several novel dementia prediction factors, including health service utilization patterns. In addition, other NIH-supported researchers developed a large language model workflow to identify cognitive concerns directly from clinical notes , with results that approached expert-level diagnostic reasoning.
While AI tools have the potential to accelerate and expand biomedical research, their ability to reason accurately across complex datasets may occasionally produce errors. To provide a method to assess the quality of the information produced by AI tools, NIH Center for Alzheimer’s and Related Dementias (CARD) researchers developed CARDBiomedBench, a 68,000-example benchmark model to evaluate how well large language models reason over real-world biomedical knowledge bases with a focus on Alzheimer’s and related dementias research. CARDBiomedBench anchors CARD.AI, an open-access AI research assistant that facilitates easy access to terabytes of data on Alzheimer’s and related dementias, including an agent to automate data prep work. Together, CARDBiomedBench and CARD.AI establish a key, transparent infrastructure for accelerating safe use of AI in Alzheimer’s and related dementias research.
Collectively, these advances reflect the growing power of AI/ML to transform how dementia is detected, diagnosed, and monitored across clinical settings. By improving diagnostic accuracy, reducing reliance on costly and often inaccessible imaging technologies, and extending validated tools to broad populations, these innovations directly support NIH's precision medicine goals. Continued investment in this rapidly evolving field holds promise for ensuring that the benefits of AI/ML-driven diagnostics reach all individuals at risk for Alzheimer's and related dementias.

## Understanding risk and protective factors for dementia

To bring us closer to preventing and treating Alzheimer's and related dementias across all populations, NIH funds research into the full range of factors that influence dementia risk, from genes and cardiovascular health to sleep, social connection, and environmental exposures. Recent findings are identifying risk factors that emerge decades before symptoms appear, offering new opportunities for early intervention and advancing the development of tailored prevention strategies.
As one example, a study supported by NIH found that physiological risk factors for Alzheimer’s, including changes in heart and immune biomarkers , were found in a large, nationally representative sample of generally healthy adults between the ages of 24 to 44 and linked to cognitive function later in life. These findings suggest that risk factors for dementia are present considerably earlier than cognitive symptoms and highlight the importance of early prevention efforts. By examining how biological, behavioral, environmental, and other factors influence dementia risk across the lifespan, NIH-funded research can empower people to take steps to reduce their own risk and guide clinicians and researchers towards earlier detection and prevention approaches.

### Biological factors

#### Genetic risk factors

In most cases, Alzheimer’s does not have a single genetic cause. Instead, it can be influenced by multiple genes in combination with other biological, environmental, and lifestyle factors. Understanding which genes play a role and what role they play may help identify new methods to prevent, delay, or treat dementia.

Nearly two-thirds of Americans living with Alzheimer’s are women. While part of the reason may be that women live longer, NIH-funded researchers are working to understand the full range of biological and environmental factors that influence women’s increased risk of dementia. Recently, NIH-supported scientists identified several genetic regions on the X-chromosome that may influence Alzheimer’s risk in women . This finding sets the stage for additional research to consider how each of these genetic regions contributes to disease risk, providing insights into disease mechanisms and possible treatment strategies.
TDP-43 is a protein that normally helps cells process RNA. In several neurodegenerative diseases, TDP-43 can become abnormal — losing its typical function in the cell nucleus and/or accumulating in clumps in nerve cells — and these changes are linked to cognitive decline. TDP-43 abnormalities often co-occur with hippocampal sclerosis (HS), which involves loss of nerve cells and scarring in a key brain region critical for memory. Together, TDP-43 pathology and HS are frequently observed in older adults with neurodegenerative disease, including frontotemporal dementia/frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTD/FTLD), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), Alzheimer's, and limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy (LATE). A team of NIH-funded investigators analyzed genetic data from thousands of brain donors and found that two genes — apolipoprotein E ( APOE ) and TMEM106B — are associated with risk for both TDP-43 pathology and HS . The researchers’ analyses further suggest that genetic factors that increase HS risk may do so partly through increasing vulnerability to TDP-43 abnormalities, which then contributes to nerve cell loss and scarring in the hippocampus. Together, these discoveries suggest that multiple neurodegenerative diseases — including Alzheimer's, LATE, ALS, and FTD — may share overlapping genetic risk pathways, supporting efforts to develop treatments that could benefit more than one condition.

#### Down syndrome and Alzheimer’s disease
One major risk factor for Alzheimer's is having Down syndrome. People with Down syndrome are born with an extra copy of chromosome 21, which causes the brain to overproduce a protein (APP) that leads to the buildup of amyloid plaques, one of the hallmarks of Alzheimer's. As a result, estimates suggest that 70% or more of people with Down syndrome will develop Alzheimer’s as they age.
To determine whether other genetic markers, in addition to the extra copy of the APP gene, affect the levels of amyloid in the blood and the brain of individuals with Down syndrome, NIH-funded scientists conducted a genome-wide association analysis using a range of amyloid blood measurements and amyloid brain scan images. They identified four novel DNA regions associated with increased levels of blood amyloid, and five novel DNA regions linked to elevated levels of amyloid in the brain of individuals with Down syndrome. Although the study was small, these findings suggest a distinct genetic architecture linked to amyloid biomarkers associated with Alzheimer’s in people with Down syndrome.
A separate investigation funded by NIH examined the brain of a woman with Down syndrome who remained cognitively healthy into her 60s, despite her brain showing the plaques and tangles that are hallmarks of Alzheimer's. Notably, she was highly educated, had a larger brain volume than most other people with Down syndrome, and showed genetic changes associated with more efficient clearance of amyloid protein. Researchers believe these factors may have given her greater cognitive reserve , essentially allowing her brain to withstand significant disease-related changes without losing function. Because this is a single case, the findings are hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive, but they may point researchers toward potential protective factors. Studying more people who remain cognitively resilient despite high genetic risk could help researchers identify what drives that resilience and, ultimately, how to replicate it.

#### Cardiovascular health

Having certain medical conditions may increase a person's risk of developing Alzheimer's or a related dementia. Cardiovascular disease, a broad category that includes conditions affecting the heart and blood vessels, such as heart failure and coronary artery disease, is one of the most studied of these risk factors. An NIH-funded study of a nationally representative group of U.S. adults over the age of 50 found that among people with high genetic risk of Alzheimer’s, better cardiovascular health was linked to reduced neurofilament protein , a marker of brain cell damage, in blood. In addition, a recent study of more than 11,000 middle-aged and older adults found that persistently taking blood pressure medication, including in individuals with the APOE4 genetic risk factor for Alzheimer’s, was linked to a lower risk of dementia . While not funded by NIH, the study leveraged data from the NIH-funded Health and Retirement Study, the largest, most comprehensive longitudinal study of aging in the U.S. These findings build on earlier results of a landmark NIH-funded clinical trial that demonstrated that intensive blood pressure control reduced the risk of mild cognitive impairment, a common precursor to dementia, and further suggest that controlling one’s blood pressure may reduce dementia risk even for individuals who have the highest genetic risk ( APOE4 carriers). Together, these findings suggest that protecting heart health across adulthood may be key to reducing risk of Alzheimer’s and related dementias.

#### Head trauma
Head trauma is another potentially modifiable risk factor for dementia, and new research is clarifying when and how it becomes dangerous. Chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) is a degenerative brain disease caused by repeated head trauma. A recent NIH-funded study found that severe, chronic CTE is associated with an increased risk for dementia , while less severe CTE was not. A separate study supported by NIH found that repeated head trauma in young athletes is associated with neuron loss and brain inflammation . Together, the studies reveal that head trauma may not be a binary risk but a progressive one, suggesting that earlier intervention could reduce dementia risk.

#### Sleep
Sleep problems may be one of the earliest warning signs of dementia risk, appearing years before any cognitive symptoms emerge. NIH-funded analyses of large health record datasets across national biobanks found that sleep disorders ─ including sleep apnea, insomnia, and circadian disruption ─ are associated with an increased risk of neurodegenerative disease up to 15 years before diagnosis . This timeline raises the possibility that disrupted sleep may actively contribute to dementia risk or may signal that harmful processes are already underway.
Other NIH-funded research is beginning to unpack the biology behind this link. Obstructive sleep apnea causes oxygen levels in the blood to drop. By monitoring older adults before, during, and after sleep, researchers found that these oxygen dips were associated with damage to small blood vessels in the brain , specifically cerebral small vessel disease, a leading cause of cognitive decline and functional loss in older adults. Together, these findings suggest that treating sleep disorders in midlife, such as with a CPAP machine for sleep apnea, could be a meaningful strategy for reducing dementia risk.

A separate study funded through NIH found that, among women in their 80s, those with an increasing sleep pattern called “24-hour sleepiness”, characterized by poor sleep at night and excessive sleepiness during the day, over five years, doubled their dementia risk during that time. The findings suggest that this sleep pattern could be an early warning sign of dementia in older women and that tracking sleep changes over time could be a practical, non-invasive way to identify those at higher risk.

Not only is poor sleep a risk factor for dementia, but good sleep, specifically sleep duration, may be a protective factor. An NIH-supported study of older women at high genetic risk of developing Alzheimer’s found that longer sleep may promote resilience to Alzheimer’s. The researchers found that, for older women with the APOE4 genetic risk factor, shorter sleep duration led to greater accumulation of the Alzheimer’s-related protein tau. However, longer sleep duration reduced this effect, suggesting that sleeping well may confer resilience to dementia despite genetic risk.

#### Menopausal transition and treatment

Women are nearly twice as likely as men to develop Alzheimer's disease. However, the biological mechanisms behind this sex difference remain unclear. NIH-funded research is exploring therapies for menopausal symptoms and age of onset as possible contributors to women’s increased risk of Alzheimer’s. In a significant NIH-funded study — led by an early-stage investigator funded by an NIH career transition grant — researchers tracked cognitively healthy women over several years and found that menopausal hormone replacement therapy in women over the age of 70 was linked to faster buildup of tau in brain regions associated with Alzheimer’s disease. In women under 70, hormone replacement therapy appeared to have little to no negative effect and may have even offered some protection.

A separate NIH-funded study found that women with an earlier age at menopause were at a higher risk for tau tangles in the brain and faster cognitive decline . However, this vulnerability was less pronounced in women with a history of hormone therapy.
While these findings do not yet support specific clinical recommendations, they suggest that timing of hormone therapy for menopause may significantly influence Alzheimer’s risk for women.

### Psychological, social, and behavioral factors
NIH also funds research exploring how psychological, social, behavioral, and environmental factors influence a person's risk for dementia. Recent studies suggest that mental health, social engagement, and lifestyle behaviors play an important role in dementia risk. For example, a study co-funded by NIH and the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) tracked over 13,000 adults for up to 15 years and found that people with a stronger sense of purpose in life were less likely to develop cognitive impairment or dementia , even after controlling for race/ethnicity, education, depression, and genetic risk for Alzheimer’s. The sense of purpose could come from any source, including relationships, volunteering, faith, hobbies, or helping others. A separate study supported by NIH found that regular time spent helping others, whether formal volunteering or helping friends and family, was linked to better cognitive function and slower cognitive decline in older adults. These findings suggest adopting behaviors to foster a sense of purpose in life, or regularly helping others, could be accessible ways to decrease the risk of dementia.

Two recent NIH-funded studies add to growing evidence that staying socially connected in older age may significantly protect against dementia. The first, following more than 12,000 adults over age 70 for up to eight years, found that loneliness was linked to a higher risk of dementia. This effect was particularly strong for women, with women who remained persistently lonely being more than twice as likely to develop dementia compared to those who never felt lonely. The second study, tracking nearly 2,000 older adults in the U.S., found that the most socially active people developed dementia about five years later than the least socially active. Together, these findings suggest that loneliness and social inactivity are not just quality-of-life concerns but meaningful risk factors for dementia, and that fostering social connection in older adults could be a powerful and cost-effective public health strategy.
NIH-funded research also found that education may shape cognitive health in older age across a variety of contexts. Using data from large, nationally representative groups of older adults from the U.S., India, and Mexico, NIH-supported scientists found that each additional year of education completed by an individual was associated with better cognitive functioning in their parents , suggesting that a family's educational environment, not just an individual's own schooling, matters for brain health. A different NIH-funded study of a nationally representative study group in India found that speaking multiple languages was linked to better cognitive functioning . Notably, cognitive benefit in executive functioning, such as thinking skills used to make plans, solve problems, and adapt to new situations, appeared even among people with no formal schooling, pointing to multilingualism itself as a possible source of cognitive protection.
Research is also revealing links between educational experiences and dementia risk later in life. For example, an NIH-funded study of a nationally representative group of older adults found that education may offer some protection from increased genetic risk of Alzheimer’s . Specifically, the study found that among Americans carrying the APOE4 gene variant, one of the strongest known genetic risk factors for Alzheimer's, those who grew up in states with greater educational resources, like higher per-pupil spending or lower student-teacher ratios, experienced slower cognitive decline compared to carriers who grew up in more poorly resourced educational environments. People without the APOE4 gene variant were largely unaffected by state educational resources. Together, these findings suggest that educational experiences may play a meaningful role in supporting brain health and reducing dementia risk.

Several NIH-funded studies point to physical activity for protecting brain health in older adults. A study of nearly 90,000 adults found that greater levels of physical activity — even in modest amounts — were consistently associated with lower dementia risk and slower cognitive decline . A separate study found that more time spent sitting was linked to smaller brain volume in Alzheimer's-related brain regions and worse memory and thinking skills over time, with the effects being particularly pronounced in people with genetic risk factors for Alzheimer's.
Another NIH-funded study found that among older adults who already had elevated amyloid in their brains, higher step counts were associated with slower cognitive decline , with benefits plateauing at a moderate goal of around 5,000 to 7,500 steps per day. Further, a clinical trial supported through NIH found that older adults with mild cognitive impairment who engaged in either moderate-intensity aerobic exercise or lower-intensity stretching and balance exercises did not experience cognitive decline . Rather, their cognition remained stable over the next year, suggesting that any form of regular, supported exercise may help prevent or slow decline.
Tobacco and cannabis use may also affect brain structure and dementia risk, according to two recent NIH-funded studies. A study that examined brain scans from more than 10,000 people found that brain regions linked to Alzheimer's, such as the hippocampus, were smaller in cigarette smokers compared to non-smokers. Also, the more a person smoked over their lifetime, the smaller the brain volume, suggesting that smoking cigarettes may contribute to dementia risk in part by accelerating brain shrinkage .
As cannabis use among older adults rises, scientists are learning about the effects of cannabis use on dementia risk. A recent study found that people aged 45 and older whose cannabis use was severe enough to require emergency or hospital care were nearly four times more likely to be diagnosed with dementia within five years compared to the general population. These findings may not generalize to typical cannabis use patterns.
Together, these findings reinforce that both smoking and heavy cannabis use are modifiable risk factors for dementia and underscore the importance of addressing substance use as part of broader brain health and dementia prevention efforts.

### Environmental factors
Recent research has uncovered a growing body of evidence that the environments in which we live may significantly shape our risk for dementia and other neurodegenerative diseases. From the air we breathe to the microscopic plastic particles now found throughout the human body, environmental exposures are emerging as potentially meaningful contributors to brain disease.
An NIH-funded study of more than 56 million U.S. Medicare beneficiaries found that long-term exposure to fine air pollution particles was significantly associated with a higher risk of Lewy body dementia . Further, laboratory experiments revealed that air pollution particles are associated with the formation of a particularly harmful form of alpha-synuclein, a misfolded brain protein which drives the brain damage seen in Lewy body dementia. A separate NIH-supported investigation of brains from 602 people found that higher air pollution particle exposure was linked to more severe Alzheimer's-related brain changes and greater cognitive impairment.
Researchers are also examining the impact of microplastics on dementia risk. Recent work has shown that small plastic particles accumulate in human organs, with the brain harboring particularly high concentrations. NIH-funded investigators found that brain tissue from people with dementia showed even greater plastic accumulation than tissue from those without the disease.
Drawing upon large biomedical datasets from the U.S. and U.K., including electronic medical records and microbiome analyses, NIH CARD researchers have mapped the intricate links between gut–brain axis dysfunction and neurodegeneration risk . These studies extend the environmental risk factor portfolio beyond air pollution and microplastics to include modifiable biological exposures along the gut–brain axis, such as those impacted by diet, stress, and other factors. Collectively, these findings suggest that our environmental exposures may meaningfully impact dementia risk.

## Identifying biological mechanisms underlying dementia risk and resilience

NIH invests in research to uncover the biological processes that underlie dementia risk and resilience, with the goal of translating observations about who develops disease into actionable strategies for prevention and treatment. Recent findings across a wide range of cellular and molecular pathways are opening new avenues for diagnostics, biomarker development, and next-generation therapies.
Knowing that a risk factor exists is only one piece of the puzzle. To drive new treatments, scientists are working to uncover what is happening at the cellular and molecular level that makes a given factor increase the risk of or protect against disease. NIH-funded research examines a wide range of biological processes, from how brain cells manage cholesterol and other fats, to how the brain flushes out waste products during sleep, to build a clearer picture of the biology underlying dementia. That deeper understanding is what makes it possible to move from observations of risk to effective prevention and treatment strategies.

### Brain waste clearance systems

The brain relies on waste clearance systems, called the lymphatic and glymphatic systems, to remove harmful proteins, including amyloid and tau, two hallmarks of Alzheimer's. A growing body of NIH-funded research suggests that breakdown of these systems may contribute to dementia. For example, researchers studying the link between traumatic brain injury (TBI) and dementia risk have found that TBI can disrupt the brain's lymphatic drainage network — the system of vessels that helps remove harmful proteins like tau from brain tissue. Emerging research in animal models identified a growth factor, VEGFC, that may promote brain drainage, reduce tau buildup, protect brain tissue, and improve cognition, pointing to a potential therapeutic strategy for preventing TBI-associated neurodegeneration.
The brain's glymphatic system, a related waste-clearance network that operates largely during sleep, also appears to play an important role in dementia risk. NIH-supported researchers found that reduced glymphatic clearance , measured using a brain imaging index called DTI-ALPS, is associated with cognitive decline. The study suggests that the DTI-ALPS score could be used as a biomarker to track disease progression in the future. In a separate NIH-funded study, researchers also found that amyloid plaques can accumulate in the small veins that drain fluid out of the brain , potentially blocking waste clearance and impairing blood flow. This finding was observed in both mouse models and postmortem human brain tissue. Taken together, these studies highlight the brain's waste clearance systems as a promising research area for understanding, detecting, and ultimately treating dementia.

### Role of cholesterol and other fats
A growing body of NIH-supported research suggests that how neurons manage cholesterol and other fats may be central to the development of dementia. One NIH-funded study found that a protein called GRAMD1B, which helps transport cholesterol, is elevated in multiple study systems: in mouse models of dementia, in lab-grown brain tissue, and in donated human brain tissue from people with Alzheimer’s or frontotemporal dementia. The researchers showed that elevated levels of this protein can cause cholesterol to accumulate inside neurons , impair the cell’s waste disposal systems, and drive the protein tau to become abnormally modified in ways associated with disease. Importantly, blocking GRAMD1B activity reversed these changes, pointing to it as a potential therapeutic target. A second NIH-funded study focused on ABCA7 , a gene linked to Alzheimer’s risk. Researchers found that when ABCA7 is dysfunctional, brain cells develop problems using certain fats (specifically one called phosphatidylcholine) important for brain structure and function, exhibit abnormal electrical activity, and overproduce amyloid. Supplementing cells with a dietary compound that boosts phosphatidylcholine production reversed many of these abnormalities.
Another NIH-funded study extended these findings to ALS and frontotemporal dementia , showing that neurons affected by the most common genetic cause of these diseases have reduced levels of important compounds known as polyunsaturated fatty acids in their cell membranes. This was observed across multiple models, from fruit flies to patient-derived stem cell neurons to postmortem human brain tissue. When researchers genetically engineered neurons to produce more of these critical compounds, fruit flies lived significantly longer and human patient neurons became more resistant to stress-induced cell death. The findings suggest that maintaining the right balance of fatty acids in neuronal membranes is critical for keeping neurons healthy, and that interventions to modify dietary intake of fatty acids (e.g., omega-3 and omega-6) could have broad relevance across multiple forms of dementia and related diseases.

### Role of cellular metabolism
How brain cells produce and use energy is increasingly recognized as central to the development and progression of dementia. One study uncovered a previously unrecognized link between tau and glycogen, a molecule used by cells to store sugar for energy. Investigators funded by NIH found that tau causes glycogen to accumulate abnormally in neurons , and that this buildup promotes further tau accumulation, creating a damaging cycle that disrupts normal cell function. In contrast, promoting the breakdown of glycogen reduced tau-related and other damage in both animal models and lab-grown neurons from people with frontotemporal dementia, suggesting that targeting glycogen metabolism could be a promising therapeutic approach. A second NIH-funded study in mice examined the role of mitochondria, the energy-generating engines of cells, finding that a deficiency in a key mitochondrial protein was sufficient to trigger Alzheimer's-like molecular changes in the brain, even in the absence of amyloid plaques or tau tangles. Treating these mice with a mitochondria-targeted drug called CP2 partially reversed these molecular changes. Notably, female mice responded more robustly than males, a finding consistent with broader evidence that biological sex shapes vulnerability to neurodegeneration.
A third study tested whether replenishing NAD+, a molecule involved in cellular energy balance and DNA repair, could reverse Alzheimer's-related damage. Researchers found that restoring healthy NAD+ levels reversed multiple hallmarks of advanced Alzheimer's in mice — including tau tangles, blood-brain barrier damage, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation — and led to cognitive recovery. Notably, postmortem brain tissue from people who had Alzheimer's-related brain changes but never developed dementia showed gene expression patterns consistent with preserved NAD+ function, suggesting that maintaining this cellular energy balance may be a key feature of resilience to the disease. Taken together, these studies identify cellular metabolism as a critical and potentially modifiable driver of neurodegeneration and point to specific molecular targets through which that dysfunction could be addressed.

### Cancer and Alzheimer’s disease

Two decades of epidemiological research have consistently demonstrated an inverse relationship between cancer and Alzheimer's: individuals with a history of cancer have a lower risk of developing Alzheimer's, and people with Alzheimer's have a lower risk of developing cancer. However, the biological mechanisms underlying this pattern have remained unclear. NIH-funded researchers have begun to identify molecular explanations for this relationship.
A team of NIH-funded researchers found that amyloid appears to play a protective role in immune cells called T cells . In mouse models, amyloid helped preserve the health and energy of T cells, improving their ability to fight tumors. Another NIH-funded study examined how a pre-malignant condition known as clonal hematopoiesis, which increases the risk of cancer, affects Alzheimer's progression. Researchers found that in mouse models, a specific form of this condition can spur an immune response that helps clear amyloid from the brain . Using population data from a major health record database, the researchers also found that this form of clonal hematopoiesis was associated with a lower risk of late-onset Alzheimer’s. These findings can help inform new strategies and opportunities for preventing and treating both cancer and Alzheimer’s.

### Dissecting disease complexity through molecular profiling
Dementia is driven by complex molecular changes that unfold over decades, and understanding those changes is essential for developing better ways to detect, prevent, and treat dementia. Advances in molecular profiling, tools that allow scientists to measure thousands of proteins, genes, and other biological signals at once, are now making it possible to map these changes at unprecedented scale and resolution.

TDP-43 plays a critical role in how cells process genetic information in the form of RNA, and TDP-43 dysfunction is a hallmark of several neurodegenerative diseases including ALS, FTD, LATE, and Alzheimer's. Four recent NIH-funded studies have converged on a previously underappreciated consequence of TDP-43 dysfunction: widespread errors in a process called alternative polyadenylation , or APA, which controls where a genetic message ends and how much of a given protein ultimately gets made. These APA errors were identified across multiple experimental models, including lab-grown human stem cell-derived neurons and postmortem brain tissue from patients . Among the affected genes, researchers identified components of a cellular transport system important for clearing toxic proteins from cells, as well as a protein called MARK3 that influences the abnormal modification of tau. Together, the findings suggest APA is a key mechanism that may drive ALS and frontotemporal dementia .
Researchers are also working to better understand selective vulnerability in the brain – that is, why certain brain regions are damaged while others are spared. A study funded by NIH combined gene expression analysis and brain imaging from postmortem tissue and identified significant differences between two brain regions with very different vulnerability to Alzheimer's: the locus coeruleus, which is highly susceptible, and the substantia nigra, which is relatively resilient (but highly vulnerable to damage in Parkinson’s disease). These differences included how each region manages cholesterol, handles oxidative stress, and responds to certain molecular signals, and may help explain why the locus coeruleus is selectively vulnerable to Alzheimer's-related degeneration. The findings point to specific biological pathways as potential targets for therapies aimed at protecting the brain's most at-risk regions.
A second NIH-supported study conducted by the ALLFTD Consortium, a collaborative program supported by NIA and NINDS, took a broad molecular profiling approach to frontotemporal dementia, finding that over 4,000 proteins were altered in the spinal fluid of 116 people carrying genetic mutations that cause the disease. Researchers identified distinct patterns of protein dysregulation tied to disease severity, including decreases in proteins associated with healthy functioning of brain cells and cellular waste clearance. These protein signatures were confirmed in independent groups of people with related conditions, suggesting they reflect reproducible features of disease biology that could serve as biomarkers or therapeutic targets. For example, NPTX2, a neuron-derived synaptic protein, revealed a strong link to cognitive decline. Combining NPTX2 with other biomarkers could help identify people at greatest risk of rapid decline and inform decisions about treatment and trial enrollment.
Two additional studies used large-scale protein profiling to explore broader patterns of molecular dysfunction across multiple neurodegenerative diseases. One NIH-funded study found that carriers of the APOE4 gene variant, one of the strongest known genetic risk factors for late-onset Alzheimer's, share a consistent pattern of changes in immune system proteins not only in Alzheimer's, but also across frontotemporal dementia, Parkinson's disease, ALS, and even in cognitively unimpaired individuals who carry the gene variant. This shared immune signature was detected across brain tissue, cerebrospinal fluid, and blood, building upon several recent publications that suggest APOE4 may act as a broad immune modulator that confers vulnerability to neurodegeneration more generally, rather than functioning as an Alzheimer's-specific risk factor. In a separate study, NIH-funded researchers coupled large-scale protein expression data with genetic information from brain regions vulnerable to Alzheimer's to identify key protein networks that are dysregulated in the disease and suggest potential targets for innovative therapeutic strategies. One protein of particular interest, called AHNAK, was experimentally validated in human stem cell-derived models of Alzheimer's and found to play a role in the interactions between different brain cell types. Together, these studies underscore the power of large-scale molecular profiling to reveal the biological underpinnings of neurodegeneration and to identify new avenues for intervention across a range of diseases.

### SPOTLIGHT: Iron and neurodegeneration

Iron is an essential mineral, but when it accumulates in the wrong places in the brain, it may contribute to the cascade of damage that leads to dementia. A growing body of research is examining how dysfunctional iron accumulation, whether driven by genetics, underlying disease, or environmental exposure, interacts with other hallmarks of neurodegeneration to drive cognitive decline.

Two studies funded by NIH examined how genetic factors and underlying disease conditions influence iron accumulation and its consequences in the brain. A large prospective study of over 12,000 healthy older adults found that a specific variant of the HFE gene, which is critical to regulating the body's iron levels, doubles the risk of developing dementia in older men . This effect was not observed in women, pointing to a potential sex-specific genetic risk factor that warrants further investigation. Notably, elevated iron levels in the blood alone did not predict dementia risk, suggesting it is the specific genetic disruption to iron regulation, rather than iron levels themselves, that matters. A separate study found that people living with Down syndrome and Alzheimer's had twice as much iron in their brains compared to cognitively normal controls, along with greater signs of cellular damage — specifically, a form of injury in which iron chemically damages the fats that make up cell membranes. Brains from people with Alzheimer's and people with Down syndrome also showed a significant loss of protective antioxidant enzymes, suggesting that the brain's defenses against iron-related damage are compromised in these conditions.
Two additional studies examined iron in the brain as a potential early warning sign of cognitive decline and as an environmental risk factor for neurodegeneration. In one study, NIH-funded researchers tracked iron deposits in the brains of cognitively normal older adults using a specific MRI technique. They found that higher brain iron levels, particularly in the entorhinal cortex, one of the first brain regions affected by Alzheimer's, predicted both the onset of mild cognitive impairment and faster cognitive decline over time , especially in individuals who also had elevated amyloid. These findings suggest that measuring brain iron could help identify people most at risk before significant cognitive damage occurs. Another NIH study raised the possibility that iron-rich air pollution may be an underrecognized source of brain iron accumulation. In a mouse study, exposure to iron oxide nanoparticles at concentrations similar to those found in underground transit systems produced strikingly different effects depending on sex: female mice developed memory impairment accompanied by increased tau, while male mice showed structural brain changes in regions associated with Parkinson's disease. Importantly, this study does not establish any causal links between exposure to iron-rich air pollution and cognitive decline in humans. Taken together, these two studies suggest that brain iron may be a significant and multifaceted contributor to neurodegeneration, influenced by genetics, disease biology, and the environment, and warranting further study.

## Advancing research on dementia care and impact of disease

As the number of people living with dementia continues to grow, so does the need and urgency for research to improve the care they receive. NIH supports studies across the full spectrum of dementia care, from how the disease shapes social cognition and daily functioning to how caregivers can be better supported. This research builds the evidence base needed to address complex care needs and improve the lives of people living with dementia and their care partners.
Dementia is one of the most pressing public health challenges of our time, carrying an enormous human and economic toll. A project funded by NIH estimates that the total economic burden of Alzheimer's and related dementias will reach $818 billion in the United States in 2026, a figure that encompasses medical costs, long-term care, and the contributions of family members and other unpaid caregivers who shoulder much of the day-to-day responsibility of supporting people living with these conditions. As the population ages and the number of people affected continues to grow, understanding and improving the full landscape of dementia care is as urgent as the need for effective therapies.
Encouragingly, the emergence of new treatments that can slow the progression of Alzheimer's is beginning to reshape what dementia care looks like in practice. A recent study using a large, nationally representative dataset found that the drug lecanemab, a treatment intended to slow cognitive decline in early Alzheimer's, generated substantial value for both patients and their care partners over time by reducing medical costs, decreasing the hours of caregiving required, and improving quality of life. Modeling projections suggested that this value grows as the treatment continues, and that next-generation therapies could more than double these benefits. These findings underscore that the value of treating dementia extends well beyond the individual, rippling outward to families, caregivers, and the broader health system.
NIH continues to fund efforts to understand which care models and programs can most benefit people living with dementia and their care partners. Two NIH-funded studies examined different approaches to structured dementia care programs. First, a trial testing an integrated palliative care approach for community-dwelling people with moderate to severe dementia found that monthly palliative care support from nurses or social workers did not significantly reduce patients' neuropsychiatric symptoms compared to usual care over a two-year follow-up. However, the palliative care group experienced less than half of the emergency department visits and hospitalizations compared to the usual care group, a finding with important implications for both patient wellbeing and healthcare costs.
Second, the D-CARE randomized clinical trial compared health system-based and community-based dementia care programs to usual care and found that neither intervention significantly improved patients' cognition, functional status, or quality of life , nor did they reduce overall caregiver burden. However, caregivers in both intervention groups reported significantly higher satisfaction with structured dementia care than those receiving usual care.

Identifying ways to better meet the needs and challenges experienced by caregivers is a key priority. An NIH-funded analysis of national survey data found that typical approaches to measure caregiver needs may not adequately identify those who are most strained . Half of eligible people with dementia were cared for by multiple caregivers, and high strain was common even among caregivers considered as providing less-intensive levels of care. In addition, use of key services such as respite care and training remained low and did not track with levels of caregiver strain, suggesting that those who need support most are not necessarily the ones receiving it. These findings have implications for the adoption and dissemination of care models in the U.S., such as Medicare’s Guiding an Improved Dementia Experience (GUIDE) model, which is examining the impact of integrated dementia care and caregiver support for people with dementia living in the community. To maximize impact, models like GUIDE should be designed to reflect the unique and often complex realities of dementia care networks, including the prevalence of multiple caregivers, varying levels of strain, and differing training needs.
Another study explored one of the most concrete and demanding aspects of caregiving: managing medications for a person living with Alzheimer's or a related dementia. Through in-depth virtual observations and interviews with 25 caregivers, NIH-funded researchers identified six key dimensions of medication management and characterized three distinct caregiver ‘personas’, each representing a different set of caregiver strategies, values, and unmet needs. These nuanced personas reveal that caregivers approach medication management in meaningfully different ways and have distinct gaps that generic tools are unlikely to address. Consideration of these personas can serve as a practical foundation for designing personalized caregiver interventions, including digital health tools, that are better matched to the diverse realities of those providing care. Together, these studies make clear that effectively supporting dementia caregivers requires moving beyond one-size-fits-all approaches and engaging with the complexity of how care is delivered in real-world scenarios.
Another study highlighted a significant and troubling gap in dementia care: most people living with dementia may not know they have it . An NIH-funded population-based study in Texas found that over 75% of older adults with probable dementia, identified through cognitive testing, reported that they had never been told their diagnosis by a healthcare provider. Importantly, being unaware of a diagnosis was not explained by lack of access to primary care or by caregiver burden, suggesting that other factors within the clinical encounter itself may be contributing to this gap. Unawareness was associated with lower cognitive test scores and greater physical impairment, raising the possibility that those most in need of support may be the least likely to know they have dementia. These findings highlight areas to improve diagnosis communication and connections to appropriate care.
One potential way to address this gap is to equip health care providers with improved tools for detecting cognitive impairment. Recently, NIH-funded researchers developed a cognitive impairment decision support system for clinicians. The investigators developed and tested the tool in 38 primary care clinics and found that the tool facilitated better care for people with mild cognitive impairment and increased provider confidence in managing care.
While significant gaps remain, these studies collectively identify concrete points of intervention to improve dementia care for all, and NIH will continue to pursue important new opportunities to strengthen the evidence base for dementia care and caregiving.

## Enhancing the research enterprise

NIH not only invests in cutting-edge basic, translational, clinical, and care research in dementia, but also funds the development and dissemination of key research resources that help advance and accelerate progress across the research enterprise. These resources benefit researchers and health care practitioners in all sectors, including academic research centers, industry, and clinical practice.
As one example, NIH-funded researchers have developed several novel models for studying Alzheimer’s and related dementias, including a 3D neurovascular model, an organoid model of traumatic brain injury, and a model incorporating all six of the major brain cell types. These models serve as powerful new tools for dementia research and can help accelerate drug development efforts. In addition, NIH recently funded two translational centers with a mission to develop microphysiologic systems, advanced cellular and tissue models that mimic human organs and disease conditions. These microphysiologic systems will be used as research tools to enable study of the complex biology of Alzheimer’s and related dementias, and advance precision medicine approaches in therapy development.
Further, NIH-funded researchers have continued to add data to and enhance the functionality and interoperability of key data and biological sample repositories, including the AD Knowledge Portal and the AgingResearchBiobank . For example, the NIH LINKAGE program connects data from 36 NIA-funded studies with data from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS). These linked datasets enable rigorous analyses of costs of care, patterns of diagnosis and treatment, and health care utilization.
Complementing these efforts, scientists with NIH CARD have developed powerful new tools to help researchers make sense of increasingly large sets of data. These tools include DIVER, which performs automated data discovery and harmonization, as well as CARD.AI, an AI-powered research assistant that helps facilitate access to terabytes of data on Alzheimer’s and related dementias, including an agent to automate critical data preparation processes. CARD researchers have also developed the CARD Catalog , a FAIR-aligned (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable) resource to help researchers identify relevant Alzheimer’s and related dementias research data, code, publications, and human cellular models. CARD is also generating a large-scale single-cell brain atlas spanning Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s disease, FTD, and neurologically normal controls to enable researchers to identify brain cell-specific vulnerabilities, resilience, and shared molecular pathways across neurodegenerative diseases. Data from this atlas will be made broadly accessible to the broader research community.
CARD also co-led the launch of the Path-NeuroDegeneration (Path-ND) consortium , a community framework for curating and harmonizing brain tissue data across major research centers and initiatives for Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, and related conditions to set data and analysis standards for evaluating digital brain tissue samples and related models.
NIH also invests in small businesses with innovative ideas for advancing dementia research. The NIA Start-Up Challenge and Accelerator provides early-stage entrepreneurs with key resources, including training, access to key industry networks, grant application skill-building, peer-to-peer networking, and individually tailored mentorship from experts across broad areas of life science entrepreneurship. Program finalists are also eligible for cash prizes intended to support early-stage research, development, and commercialization activities. 2025 finalists included several small businesses developing therapeutics and drug discovery platforms to prevent and treat Alzheimer’s and other neurodegenerative diseases.
NIH also supports efforts to develop the workforce needed to conduct basic, translational, and clinical research to prevent and treat dementia, as well as enhance the workforce of people caring for individuals living with dementia. For example, NIH and the Alzheimer’s Association jointly fund the Institute on Methods and Protocols for Advancement of Clinical Trials in Alzheimer’s and Related Dementias , which aims to expand the clinical trials workforce. More than 230 participants, including early-stage investigators and those new to dementia trials, have completed this comprehensive training program in conducting clinical trials for Alzheimer’s and related dementias, including 42 in the 2025 cohort. In addition, NIH funded more than 170 early career investigators in Alzheimer’s and related dementias research via fellowship programs, as well as more than 280 recipients of career development awards to early career dementia researchers, in fiscal year 2025.
Further, NIH aims to develop the next generation of dementia researchers by funding several initiatives to provide training and research opportunities for high school, undergraduate, and postbaccalaureate students and science teachers. Two of NIH’s signature programs are the Expanding Research in Alzheimer’s and Related Dementias (ERA) research education programs. The first program is for high school students, undergraduate students, and science teachers, while the second program serves postbaccalaureate students. More than 30 trainees received support via these programs, focused on dementia research, in fiscal year 2025. In addition, NIH’s Research and Entrepreneurial Development Immersion program helps prepare biomedical researchers for the full spectrum of modern career options beyond tenure-track faculty positions. In fiscal year 2025, NIH supported nearly 80 trainees focused on careers in dementia research.

## Looking forward

This report highlights the significant progress in dementia research in 2025. These results build upon previous achievements in recent years, thanks to sustained investments that have supported broad work across the research pipeline, from basic to translational to clinical research. In addition, the emergence of new tools like AI/ML is accelerating biomedical research by enabling new and more efficient analyses of large datasets, informing fresh approaches in identifying new targets and strategies for preventing and treating Alzheimer’s and related dementias, and offering potential new avenues for care and caregiving research. NIH remains committed to advancing dementia research and building on the tremendous progress of recent years to bring America closer to a future shaped by more effective prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and care/caregiving strategies for Alzheimer’s and related dementias.
